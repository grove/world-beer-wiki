# American beer: domestic labor, immigration and the rise of industry

The American beer story is not simply a technological march from ale to lager. The Smithsonian National Museum of American History's beer-brewing archive describes domestic fermentation by women, enslaved people and other workers, and the later rise of immigrant-led professional lager brewing. That is a history of labor, migration and changing institutions as much as one of recipes.[^v114-smithsonian]

The finding aid also notes Indigenous fermented beverages in North America made from different local plants before colonial brewing techniques arrived. An institutional summary is a useful orientation, but it must not collapse those beverages into European malt beer, or imply that their production disappeared when lager became industrially successful.[^v114-smithsonian]

The collection includes oral histories and objects that could reveal individual brewers' lived experiences. **We did not read or listen to those individual interviews.** A finding aid is not a transcription, and the archive's aggregate explanation should not be placed in the mouth of a named interview participant. The distinction matters particularly when discussing workers historically omitted from commercial brand histories.[^v114-smithsonian]

A modern category page can explain a finished beer's style, but it cannot substitute for an account of who made and served beer at different moments.[^v114-smithsonian]
