# When did Bere reach Orkney? A long crop history is not a dated genealogy

## Ancient barley is not automatically the present landrace

Agriculture and barley cultivation have a long archaeological history on Orkney. But Martin and colleagues' account of Bere is careful about the history of the named landrace: its precise introduction to Scotland remains uncertain, even though Bere has survived as a locally important six-row barley and was once more widespread in northern Britain.[^v117-bere-review]

The University of the Highlands and Islands describes Bere as a historic crop and part of current agronomic and market research. That institutional description identifies conservation and cultivation work; it is not a dated archaeobotanical demonstration that a particular present-day genetic population was used in a Neolithic beer.[^v117-bere-institute]

## Three different timelines

One can date archaeological barley remains, identify the period of a written crop name, or document the continuity of living cultivation and product uses. These sources can overlap without proving exact lineage, survival of an identical seed population or the recipe of every historical brewer. A sixth-century or Neolithic grain record cannot supply modern sensory descriptors for a named Bere product.

For an evidence-based history, state what is an excavated plant remain, what is inferred from a crop-history review, and what is observed in current farming. Claiming that every present island farmer keeps a single unchanged ancient recipe would require much stronger local-language and archival work.

This guide does not assign an exact first-growing day or make a recurring beer festival from an approximate historical period.
