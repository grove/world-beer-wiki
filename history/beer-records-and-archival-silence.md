# Who is missing from brewing archives?

An evidence-method reference comparing medieval written rules with contemporary oral-history collections; not a reconstructed account of unnamed individuals.

## Documentation follows institutions

Regulation, taxation and court disputes leave identifiable names, while many unlicensed or short-lived ventures remain harder to trace. The Museum of Oxford explicitly warns that small-scale brewing can fall outside surviving records. An archive of brewers is therefore not equivalent to a census of all brewing labor.[^v15-oxford-alewives]

## Oral history changes the question

The Smithsonian’s American Beer Brewing Oral History Collection documents narrators describing brewery operations, entrepreneurship, homebrewing and identity between 2017 and 2023. Such evidence can illuminate experiences absent from sales ledgers, but interviews also reflect memory, selection and interviewer context.[^v15-smithsonian-oral]

## A practical source rule

When the wiki makes claims about who brewed, record whether the evidence is a law, a named interview, an excavation or an analyst’s reconstruction. Do not use absence from one source to assert that a group did not participate; do not assume an archival catalog description supplies everything said in a transcript.[^v15-bennett-book][^v15-smithsonian-oral]

## Edition 1.9: newly sourced connections

The Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative describes an actual Ur III administrative tablet giving two named beer forms as part of provisions for three messengers. This is excellent evidence of **recorded allocations and bureaucratic classification**, but not an everyday menu or recipe. In particular, the catalog explains that beer compensation could occur on special service rather than as a regular ration for every worker. The example sharpens a broader source-method issue: what ancient states chose to record was not everything that people drank or knew. Read the [tablet interpretation](../history/ur-iii-beer-ration-tablet.md) and [the tablet as an object](../objects/ur-iii-beer-tablet-as-object.md) before extrapolating to a population-wide beer wage.[^v19-cdli-ration]

## Edition 1.11: newly sourced connections

Mexican historical evidence shows why *who recorded a drink* matters. A scholar working from clerical transcriptions investigates first-pulque ritual representations; another interprets Chalco tax administration during 1778–1821. Church observers and revenue officials left records for different purposes. Neither archive is a neutral census of all Nahua or pulque-making households.[^v111-pulque-ritual][^v111-pulque-tax]

Follow [Nahua first-pulque records](../culture/nahua-first-pulque-ritual-in-colonial-records.md) and [Chalco taxes](chalco-pulque-taxation-1778-1821.md). The studies' public abstracts, rather than transcribed primary documents, were checked; ritual details and tax figures are not reconstructed without fuller evidence.
