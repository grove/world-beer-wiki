# When hop kilns became heritage buildings

A kiln that no longer dries hops can still preserve evidence of how beer ingredients were supplied. Historic England inventories many oast houses and hop kilns and documents their changing distribution, forms and working arrangements. By the late nineteenth century, English hop production had concentrated in particular regions; surviving buildings are a partial record of that shifting geography.[^v114-oasts]

The article distinguishes the kiln from the *stowage*, where dried hops cooled and could be packed. The special profile of a Kent oast with a cowl is a practical response to ventilation, not an independent indicator of what kind of beer the hops eventually flavored. A modern photograph of a converted oast cannot prove its original date, furnace conditions or yearly throughput without building records.[^v114-oasts]

Malt kilns and malt houses belong beside this history, but their products and heat processes are not interchangeable. One deals with the grain substrate after germination; the other with freshly picked hop cones. Historic England treats both as distinct forms of food-processing industrial heritage, and a brewery historian needs both to understand the long chain between field and pint.[^v114-malthouses]

Architectural survival is evidence of buildings and changing work, not a chemical record of a historical recipe.[^v114-oasts]
