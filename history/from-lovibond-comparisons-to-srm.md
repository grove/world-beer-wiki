# From visual colour comparisons to instrumental SRM

## Why colour needed a convention

A visual statement such as “amber” conveys something useful but depends on the observer and the viewing conditions. The BJCP's technical colour guide describes beer SRM as a defined light-absorbance measurement and explains its relation to EBC reporting. Its printable or physical colour comparison aid is an approximation intended to make the scale useful in sensory settings.[^v116-color]

Earlier brewing descriptions frequently used visually identified colours or ingredient-oriented categories, yet these terms were neither single fixed recipes nor the same as an optical value. The present sources do not constitute an archival history of every Lovibond glass standard or the dates of every laboratory's transition to spectroscopy.

## A metric has a method

Thesseling and colleagues' open laboratory account describes spectrophotometric colour determination on a prepared sample. That illustrates the distinction between a standardized contemporary optical reading and the historical experience of a beer observed under ordinary light.[^v116-lab]

The BJCP's malt guide links malt choices and wort processing to beer colour. A retrospective assignment of today's SRM number to an undocumented historic batch would require evidence beyond the style's name or the colour of one named ingredient.[^v116-malt]

## Preserve the source vocabulary

If an eighteenth-century record says “pale,” quote or paraphrase it as a historical description. If a modern analysis reports a wavelength, path length and SRM, record that measured method. Avoid converting historical descriptive words to exact technical ranges by guesswork.
