# Qasr Ibrim barley: ancient DNA and grain diversity

The archaeological barley of Qasr Ibrim offers an unusually instructive caution for beer-history research. Genetic work on ancient grains investigated a two-row appearance associated with an allele descended from six-row barley. The study’s *vrs1* analysis addresses crop morphology and local adaptation hypotheses, rather than testing a beer, a fermentation vessel or a finished beverage.[^v19-nubian-barley]

## Grain categories can hide history

Barley is often sorted today into two-row and six-row types in brewing discussions. Genetic studies show that a visible row-type trait is not always a simple statement of ancestry. Qasr Ibrim material can illuminate evolution and selection in ancient agriculture, but it cannot identify the malt quality, the exact grain ingredients of nearby beer or a regional style that survived to the present.[^v19-nubian-barley]

## What a useful beer-wiki link should say

A grain from an archaeological layer becomes evidence of **available plant material in that context**, with dating and contamination questions of its own. To argue for brewing, an investigator would need additional independent evidence such as residues, installations, or appropriately interpreted documents. The finding is valuable without converting a crop genetics paper into a brewing excavation report.[^v19-nubian-barley]
