# Chicha in Chilean colonial records: whose voice do we hear?

A 2024 scholarly review of fermented beverages in pre-Hispanic Chile quotes or discusses historical chroniclers describing chicha consumption and preparation. Such texts can be important records of **what an observer wrote**, but colonial authors' moral judgments and political interests may affect the language of their testimony.[^v16-chile-review]

## Evidence versus inherited narrative

The review discusses women's roles in making and sharing some fermented beverages, alongside political hospitality and ritual settings. These claims need place, time and source boundaries. A colonial comment about “the natives” is not a community-wide self-description, nor does it establish unbroken continuity to all current Mapuche or Andean practices.[^v16-chile-review]

## An adjacent but different modern study

A 2026 review of chicha de jora in Peru synthesizes different maize varieties, processing choices and microbial consortia. It can help compare questions about starch conversion and fermentation, but it is not a primary source for what a seventeenth-century Chilean chronicler witnessed.[^v16-chicha-2026]

## How to improve the archive

Prioritize Indigenous-authored scholarship, original-language examination, dated primary documents and explicit attribution. Do not upgrade repeated colonial anecdotes into universal beer origin facts. Link historical interpretation to named communities and their present voices when those are publicly and permissibly documented.
