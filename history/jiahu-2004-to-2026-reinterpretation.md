# Jiahu archaeology: what changed between 2004 and 2026?

## The original chemical interpretation

McGovern and colleagues’ 2004 article examined organic residues absorbed into Jiahu pottery and argued for a mixed fermented drink involving rice, honey and fruit. Its strength was a combination of residue chemistry and site context; its limits included uncertainty about which specific fruits were used and the inability to recover the beverage itself. The paper describes a deeply ancient food technology, not a preserved recipe with modern ingredient quantities.[^v113-jiahu-2004]

## New microfossils changed the process hypothesis

A 2026 research group revisited residues from Jiahu pottery using microfossil methods and reported starch damage and fungal features interpreted as mold-assisted saccharification, alongside signatures compatible with malted cereal processing. It also broadened the possible plant inputs indicated by starch and phytolith data. In their account, qu-like conversion may have been more central than the 2004 chemical narrative suggested; the reinterpretation is a research claim, not a recovered ancient instruction book.[^v113-jiahu-2026]

## Why both papers belong in the wiki

Chemical biomarkers, starch microstructures and archaeological vessel context answer related but different questions. Contradiction is not necessarily error: additional methods may support alternatives that were previously invisible. Readers should ask which materials were detected, which processes were inferred, and how strongly each result depends on comparison with modern fermentation. Neither study establishes direct descent from a present-day rice beer or proof that all Jiahu vessels held identical contents.[^v113-jiahu-2004][^v113-jiahu-2026]
