# How archaeologists infer beer without finding a recipe

## Beer does not fossilize as a pint

Ancient cereal fermentation is reconstructed from indirect traces. Mijiaya's investigators paired starch and phytolith remains with vessel morphology and chemical evidence. Shangshan's team combined plant proxies and fungal structures in pottery context; Raqefet researchers used residue, wear and experimental comparison. Multiple lines of evidence strengthen a proposal, but they do not become a surviving recipe.[^v13-mijiaya][^v13-shangshan][^v13-raqefet]

## Different sources support different questions

A dated burial assemblage can locate an event but not name the brewers; starch damage may suggest processing but not a precise temperature; fungal morphology may resemble known starters but not reveal the full living microbial community. Experimental reconstructions test a possible pathway, not necessarily the historical pathway. 'Earliest known' claims must specify geography, analytical method and date range.[^v13-shangshan][^v13-raqefet]

## Edition 1.4: Converging residue evidence

An open study of Early Neolithic Chinese vessels combines starch, phytolith and fungal residues to infer differing saccharification methods, while explicitly noting that human mastication cannot be established from the available material. This illustrates how evidence strength and gaps should be reported. See [the case study](early-neolithic-malts-and-starters.md).[^v14-neolithic]

## Research extension 1.5: From vessel residue to political production

At Cerro Baúl, excavators assembled architectural, botanical and equipment evidence for a large brewery, while a later study compared experimental production with excavated deposits. The combination demonstrates why one microscopic particle or vessel shape is less persuasive than a converging archaeological context.[^v15-wari-2005][^v15-wari-2024]

Explore the [excavated brewery](cerro-baul-wari-brewery.md) and [what experimental reconstructions can test](../processes/archaeological-brewing-reconstruction.md).


## Edition 1.9: newly sourced connections

Ancient barley genetics at Qasr Ibrim show why **finding a relevant cereal is not the same as finding beer**. The study examined grain row-type ancestry and the *vrs1* locus; its results support questions about crop adaptation, not a particular fermentation or alcohol content. Such a claim would need additional chemical residues, installations or appropriately interpreted texts. Read [the Qasr Ibrim analysis](qasr-ibrim-barley-ancient-dna.md) and [two-row / six-row barley cautions](../ingredients/barley-row-type-and-brewing-assumptions.md) as a methodological check against turning agricultural archaeology into an invented brewing tradition.[^v19-nubian-barley]

## Edition 1.13: newly sourced connections

Jiahu now offers a useful methodological dialogue: a 2004 chemical-residue reconstruction argued for a mixed drink of rice, honey and fruit; a 2026 microfossil study revisits the same site and proposes a more central role for mold-assisted starch conversion. Neither analysis yields a complete ancient recipe, and the different signals should remain separately attributed.[^v113-jiahu-2004][^v113-jiahu-2026]

See [the two-paper historical comparison](jiahu-2004-to-2026-reinterpretation.md) and [the sampled pottery](../objects/jiahu-pottery-residue-sampling.md).

## Edition 1.6: sourced research connections

The University of Copenhagen's FERMENTED PASTS project proposes comparing historical brewing objects with current microbial lineages using ancient DNA and microbiological methods. This is an excellent **research question**, not proof that a viable ancient yeast has been recovered from a ring or barrel. Residue attribution, contamination controls, dating and contextual artifacts remain necessary to make an archaeological claim.[^v16-fermented-pasts]
