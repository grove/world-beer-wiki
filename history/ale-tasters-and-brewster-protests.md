# Aletasters, price controls and brewsters’ resistance

A medieval ale sale could leave evidence in a court record because local officers checked prices and commercial rules. Judith Bennett argues that this regulation is one reason historians can reconstruct women’s brewing labor unusually well: authorities recorded transactions, alleged breaches and disputes. The resulting documents are valuable but biased toward activities officials wanted to police.[^v17-bennett-law]

## Two named moments, not the whole population

Bennett’s chapter abstract describes a brewer named Gillian, wife of Richard Pykard, objecting to aletasters at Wakefield in 1275. It also recounts Exeter brewsters who, dissatisfied with an enforced price in 1317, took down their signs and stopped selling. These cases illustrate negotiation and occasional collective resistance—not a general strike by every brewer in England.[^v17-bennett-law]

## Regulation was more than hygiene

Price controls protected certain market expectations and produced income for local authorities. Rules also shaped who could sell and under what conditions. A citation to an aletaster therefore does not prove that the official performed a modern laboratory safety assessment. “Quality,” price and licensing had historical meanings that need their own context.[^v17-bennett-law]
