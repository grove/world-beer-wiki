# Norwegian beer exports, natural ice and shipping between 1860 and 1900

## A useful archive can show the infrastructure behind beer trade

Norwegian Museum of Science and Technology curator Ingebjørg Eidhammer summarizes an archival project drawing on Ringnes, Frydenlund and Schous brewery records. The museum describes beer exports during **1860–1900** and the importance of Norway's natural ice, maritime fleet, sample shipments, steamships and new railway infrastructure. It places this within the emergence of technical and microbiological improvements during industrialization.[^v119-norwayice]

An independent University of Chicago Library exhibition gives a broader context for how natural ice and mechanical cooling changed the possible geography of beer storage and shipping. It does not supply counts of Norwegian shipments, the quality of every exported batch or the commercial agreements of individual captains.[^v119-chicagoice]

## Export does not establish a single global style

A brewery label, sample sent by ship and archival business correspondence answer different questions. Shipping a beer long distances is not proof that it tasted the same at arrival, that foreign customers adopted the same social practices, or that the exported drink contained a single universal yeast strain. The archival source itself distinguishes enabling technologies such as transport and cooling from the beverage's local identity.

Record brewery, ports, approximate period, evidence kind and any preserved labels with image-rights caution. The 1860–1900 span is a historical period, not one exact national festival or current schedule.
