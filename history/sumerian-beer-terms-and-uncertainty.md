# Sumerian beer words are not modern style labels

Sumerian beer history is unusually well documented in tablets, but that abundance does not yield a simple list of modern styles. Peter Damerow distinguishes administrative records, literary compositions and material remains, explaining how each can reveal a different part of production and consumption. The many named ingredients and products are linguistically difficult: a familiar modern translation may conceal uncertainty about the original operation.[^v19-damerow]

## The trap of a smooth recipe

The *Hymn to Ninkasi* is a poetic work known through later copies. Its passages about grain, preparation, fermentation and pouring are historically important, but a neat sequence can emerge when translators already assume how brewing must work. Damerow questions whether particular words identify malt, cooked mash, a dried intermediate product or a different local technology. The question is not whether fermentation occurred; it is how much detail the texts can uniquely fix.[^v19-damerow]

## Records show differentiation, not genealogies

The CDLI description of a Ur III messenger tablet names two forms of beer in rations. One archive’s categories, and any modern English rendering, cannot show that an ancient drink is the direct ancestor of porter, wheat beer or another present judging class. Nor does a tablet proving commerce establish what all contemporaries preferred or consumed.[^v19-cdli-ration]
