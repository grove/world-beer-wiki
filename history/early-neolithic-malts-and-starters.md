# Two approaches to starch conversion in Early Neolithic China

An open archaeological study of 8,000–7,000-year-old pottery residues in northern China describes evidence consistent with both sprouted-grain starch conversion and mold-based starters. These methods solve the same biochemical problem in different ways.[^v14-neolithic]

## Evidence from a vessel

Researchers combined starch granules, phytoliths, fungi and vessel form. They argue for a role of specialized globular jars and mixed plant materials in early fermentation. The results document the sampled artifacts; they cannot tell us which modern drink a Neolithic individual would recognize.[^v14-neolithic]

## An absence is not a proof

The authors discuss possible salivary starch conversion but explicitly state that the existing data do not establish it. This is an example of why the [archaeological method guide](how-archaeologists-identify-beer.md) and [mold starter](../ingredients/mould-and-mixed-starters.md) references should separate detection, interpretation and later textual accounts.[^v14-neolithic]

## Edition 1.13: newly sourced connections

The 2026 Jiahu microfossil study argues for qu-like fungal saccharification plus indications compatible with malt-related processing in its Neolithic pottery. These are proxy-based reconstructions, not identification of a preserved living inoculum or the origin of all later East Asian grain ferments.[^v113-jiahu-2026]
