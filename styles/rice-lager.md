# Rice Lager

Rice Lager names a lager in which rice is a meaningful part of the fermentable ingredients. The Brewers Association added a named Rice Lager style for its 2026 edition while explicitly noting that brewing rice lagers was not new.[^modern-ba-2026-release] A new guideline entry dates recognition within that system, not invention of the beverage.

## Ingredient and sensory scope

The 2026 BA entry uses a minimum 15% rice criterion and keeps hop character subordinate; it allows rice expression ranging from neutral to noticeable.[^modern-ba-2026] These are boundaries of that particular judging description, not a universal rice percentage for everything sold under the name.

Echigo's Koshihikari beer is a concrete Japanese example. Its producer identifies Niigata Koshihikari rice, lists barley malt, wheat malt, hops and rice, and describes a crisp lager with a smooth finish. The listing gives 5% ABV. This mixed-grain formulation is important: “rice beer” does not, by itself, mean a beer made exclusively from rice.[^modern-echigo-rice]

## Keep three identities separate

Use the ingredient name, the commercial style label and the cultural tradition as separate fields. This page covers a contemporary lager category. It should not absorb every rice-based ferment in the wiki, such as [sake](sake.md), [makgeolli](makgeolli.md), [sato](sato.md) or [Judima](judima.md). Their own pages describe their names, starters and contexts.

Likewise, do not infer “Japanese” from rice alone or infer rice from every Japanese lager label. The source of a grain and the location of a brewery answer different questions. Echigo's explicitly declared ingredients support a statement about that product, not every beer from the country.

## Classification

BJCP suggests 2A International Pale Lager for Japanese Rice Lager.[^modern-bjcp-entry-suggestions]

The wording is narrower than a universal mapping for all rice lagers. Record the guide, edition and exact label when comparing this with the BA category.

## Evidence note

No ancient origin, first brewer, universal flavor or gluten claim follows from the category name. The evidence here supports a modern classification and a named producer example.
