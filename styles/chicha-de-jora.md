# Chicha de jora

**Beer Tradition** · South America / Peru / Andes · living tradition

Chicha de jora is a Peruvian maize-beer tradition, not a single standardized recipe. Its defining starting material is sprouted maize, called jora. The cited study documents contemporary artisanal production and places it within a much older Andean brewing tradition.[^chicha-jora-study]

## Character

The source describes a yellowish, lightly alcoholic, effervescent drink. Individual producers may add other grains, sugar, herbs, spices, or fruit. This diversity makes a single sweetness or flavor description less useful than identifying the producer and ingredients.[^chicha-jora-study]

## Brewing and context

Maize is germinated, dried, ground, and cooked with water before fermentation. The study describes fermentation vessels and the reuse of material from earlier batches. Its microbiological survey found substantial differences between producers, including communities of lactic-acid and acetic-acid bacteria.[^chicha-jora-study]

## History and interpretation

The researchers examined 27 samples from 14 producers in seven Peruvian provinces. That is evidence of real variation, not permission to describe every Andean drink called chicha as identical. This entry concerns maize-based chicha de jora specifically; it does not absorb cassava-based traditions under the same name.[^chicha-jora-study]

## What archaeology can and cannot add

Excavation at the Wari site of Cerro Baúl documented a large brewing facility with areas interpreted as milling, boiling, and fermentation spaces. The 2005 report also identified abundant *Schinus molle* remains. The authors explicitly left open whether maize and molle drinks were brewed separately or combined.[^trad-chicha-archaeology]

That ambiguity matters. A report about ancient **chicha** is not automatically a recovered recipe for modern **chicha de jora**. Archaeological brewing installations can support an account of production and social organization while leaving ingredients, mixtures, and sensory character partly unresolved.[^trad-chicha-archaeology]

## A useful distinction when indexing chicha

Use this page for the named sprouted-maize tradition. Keep molle-based drinks, other regional uses of “chicha,” and cassava ferments in their own evidential context. Likewise, a modern reconstruction should be described as a reconstruction and linked to the evidence it interprets, rather than presented as an unchanged ancient beverage.

## Evidence note

The contemporary microbiological study and the archaeological paper address different periods and questions. The former concerns sampled Peruvian production; the latter concerns a specific Wari installation and includes explicit ingredient uncertainty. Neither establishes direct continuity of one recipe across those settings.

The archaeological account of Chimu-period Manchan links chicha production with provisioning and reciprocal obligations in a particular North Coast setting. ## Research extension 1.5: Wari ceramic evidence complicates any single chicha recipe

Excavation at Cerro Baúl documents a monumental Wari brewing operation where sprouted maize indicators, molle drupes, hearths and fermentation vessels occur together. The researchers explicitly leave open whether maize and molle were brewed separately or combined. This is archaeological evidence for site-specific production, not permission to treat one excavation as the authentic recipe of all Andean chicha.[^v15-wari-2005]

A later study using excavated dregs and informant-guided experiments argues that Wari chicha production in the studied Moquegua province was largely organized through specialized facilities. The result contrasts with an easy assumption that all Wari households independently brewed the same beverage.[^v15-wari-2024]

## Research extension 1.3: Germinated maize and processing variability

A scientific synthesis of **chicha de jora** describes germinated maize, drying, cooking and fermentation as variable stages in documented Peruvian production. This materially differs from simply putting unmalted maize into an industrial lager. The [jora ingredient reference](../ingredients/jora-maize.md) and [starch conversion](../processes/starch-conversion.md) explain the distinction without declaring one archaeological recipe.[^v13-chicha]

## Edition 1.6: sourced research connections

A 2026 Peruvian scholarly review describes chicha de jora as a **variable cereal fermentation system**, compiling maize varieties, adjuncts, different processing sequences and reported microbial assemblages. The review strengthens the case against treating one industrial recipe as the ancient or nationally compulsory form. Its discussion of possible bioactive components does not establish clinical benefit.[^v16-chicha-2026]
