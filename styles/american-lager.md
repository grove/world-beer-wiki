# American Lager

**Beer Style** · North America · contemporary

American lager is the familiar pale, highly carbonated lager associated with large American breweries. It emphasizes a smooth, neutral taste rather than pronounced hop bitterness, rich toast, or fruity fermentation.[^bjcp-2021-1b]

## Character

The flavor is usually lightly grainy, with a dry to gently rounded finish. Barley malt may be combined with maize or rice. Those ingredients are part of the style's brewing history, not automatic evidence that a beer is defective.[^bjcp-2021-1b]

## Brewing and context

Cool fermentation and lager maturation help produce its clean profile. Compared with American light lager, the standard version generally has more body and flavor. Compared with older American pilsner interpretations, it usually has less prominent hopping.[^bjcp-2021-1b]

## History and interpretation

The modern style developed through changes in American brewing and consumption, including the periods surrounding Prohibition and the Second World War. A current example should not be treated as an unchanged nineteenth-century recipe. Nor does this category describe every lager brewed in the United States.[^bjcp-2021-1b]

## Evidence note

This profile paraphrases a modern judging reference. Its flavor description is better supported than any claim of a single origin or an unchanged historical recipe. It is not a complete brewing recipe.

## Edition 1.14: newly sourced connections

The Smithsonian's beer-brewing collection frames the spread of professional lager production within **nineteenth-century immigration and industrialization**, rather than as a purely sensory improvement or an inevitable single invention. Earlier brewing work included domestic labor by women, enslaved workers and other people often omitted from commercial brand narratives.[^v114-smithsonian]

A modern American lager category cannot substitute for the changing social history of who brewed and sold beer. The archive's collection-level finding aid is an orientation source, not evidence from specific interviews we read. Compare [American labor and immigration](../history/american-beer-households-immigration-and-industry.md) and [recorded brewing voices](../culture/american-beer-oral-histories.md).[^v114-smithsonian]
