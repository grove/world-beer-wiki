# Sake

**Related Fermented-Grain Tradition** · East Asia / Japan · living tradition

Sake is a Japanese rice-brewing tradition. It belongs in this wiki as a neighboring grain-fermented beverage, not because calling it “rice wine” or “rice beer” captures its full identity. This introductory entry focuses on the starch-conversion method described by a working brewery.[^sake-producer]

## Character

The cited producer account is about making koji, not a tasting survey. It is therefore insufficient to assign all sake a single sweetness, aroma, or texture. This file deliberately avoids treating one brewery's production explanation as a definition of every sake category.[^sake-producer]

## Brewing and context

Rice is steamed and some is inoculated with koji mold under controlled conditions. The mold supplies the means to turn rice starch into sugars that yeast can ferment. That is different from relying on enzymes developed by germinating barley for conventional malt beer.[^sake-producer]

## History and interpretation

The brewery describes careful cooling, temperature monitoring, and handling of the rice. These are observations from contemporary production, not evidence for an unchanged ancient recipe. A fuller taxonomy of sake would need separate research into production categories and terminology; it is not hidden inside this beer-style overview.[^sake-producer]

## Evidence note

The description is limited to the sources below. A local observation or laboratory sample does not establish one recipe, strength, or flavor for every community or period.

## Edition 1.7: newly sourced connections

In 2024 UNESCO inscribed the traditional knowledge and skills of sake-making with koji mold in Japan. The nomination description emphasizes craft transmission through *tōji* and *kurabito*, fermentation knowledge, festivals, weddings and cooperation with farmers. It is recognition of a *practice*, not an official mandate to classify sake as beer.[^v17-unesco-sake]

The [heritage case study](../culture/japanese-koji-sake-heritage-and-apprenticeship.md) and [rice-ferment comparison](../categories/koji-sake-and-rice-ferment-boundaries.md) place the biochemical and social dimensions together. UNESCO’s indexed description does not resolve every regional recipe or imply unbroken ancient continuity.


## Edition 1.9: newly sourced connections

Chinese Huangjiu provides a useful comparison because both sake and Huangjiu require an external or microbial route for cereal starch to become fermentable sugars. But a 2022 Huangjiu review describes its own regional rice and wheat-*qu* systems; it does not show that Japanese sake uses an identical starter, identical phase timing, or a proven single line of invention. Read the [Huangjiu profile](huangjiu-chinese-cereal-wine.md) and [rice-wine category boundaries](../categories/rice-wine-beer-and-distillation-boundaries.md). The community's name and method should remain primary; an English “rice wine” translation is not a taxonomy.[^v19-huangjiu-review]


## Edition 1.12: newly sourced connections

A Japanese sake-producer-authored technical review distinguishes **koji amazake**, in which koji enzymes sweeten a grain substrate, from **sake-lees amazake**, in which sake-production material is reused and residual alcohol may remain. The name *amazake* should therefore not be treated as synonymous with finished alcoholic sake or with universal zero-alcohol content.[^v112-amazake-2021] Read [Amazake](amazake.md), [koji saccharification](../processes/koji-amazake-saccharification.md), and [food roles](../food/amazake-sugar-and-sake-lees.md).
