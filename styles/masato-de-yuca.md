# Masato de yuca

**Related Starch-Fermentation Tradition** · South America / Peru / Amazonia · living tradition

Masato de yuca is a cassava-based fermented drink associated with Peruvian Amazonian communities. It belongs beside beer in a comparative fermentation wiki, but cassava is a root crop, not a cereal. The name and local context should therefore be retained rather than replaced with “maize beer.”[^masato-study]

## Character

The cited research focused on microorganisms rather than a complete tasting description. It does not establish one color, sweetness, texture, or alcohol level for all masato. This profile leaves those characteristics open rather than borrowing them from another regional drink.[^masato-study]

## Brewing and context

The researchers sampled six commercial producers in Chanchamayo and Satipo. Their masato contained several kinds of lactic-acid bacteria. Importantly, the sampled commercial preparations did not use human saliva; chewing must not be presented as a universal requirement of the tradition.[^masato-study]

## History and interpretation

The paper concerns local fermentation practices and laboratory screening of bacterial strains. Neither its small sample nor its search for potentially useful organisms proves a universal recipe or a health benefit from drinking masato. The entry is deliberately scoped to the documented Peruvian cassava tradition.[^masato-study]

## Evidence note

The description is limited to the sources below. A local observation or laboratory sample does not establish one recipe, strength, or flavor for every community or period.

## Edition 1.8: newly sourced connections

A community-partnered study of one Shawi community in the Peruvian Amazon describes household cassava preparation and daily service using participatory photographs. Its documented process included chewing some cooked cassava, but the commercial-producer study cited earlier in this profile involved **no saliva**. Neither source describes every community called Amazonian or every preparation named masato.[^v18-shawi]

The Shawi study also cultured **26 communal water-source samples and 11 masato samples**, with coliform detection in all water samples and two masato samples. These exploratory results are **not** proof of microbiological safety, a clinical outcome, or a water-treatment recipe. The authors requested further investigation.[^v18-shawi]

Explore [the Shawi account](../culture/shawi-masato-and-water.md), [photographed preparation stages](../processes/shawi-masato-preparation.md), and [why the safety inference is limited](../stories/fermentation-is-not-a-water-treatment.md).

## Edition 1.10: newly sourced connections

A 2013 ethnographic discussion of Yaminahua *masateadas* shows that a named manioc drink can carry meaning through visits, song, family ties and social tension. The source discusses the social gathering in a specific Peruvian Amazonian setting, not a fixed annual festival or standardized method shared by all masato makers.[^v110-yaminahua]

The wiki also discusses community-partnered Shawi research, whose methods and questions are different. Compare the [Yaminahua gathering account](../culture/yaminahua-masateadas-and-kinship.md) with the [Shawi photography and water study](../culture/shawi-masato-and-water.md); shared beverage terminology should not erase distinct participants or treat ethnography as microbial evidence.


## Edition 1.11: newly sourced connections

Brazilian [cauim](cauim.md) and [giroba](giroba.md) belong in the comparison network, but shared cassava and fermentation do **not** make them alternate names for the Shawi or other Peruvian masato documented here. A USP archaeological-historical account discusses Guarani and Tupinambá sources; a different ethnography examines food and sociality among Tupinambá de Olivença. Those cannot be copied into a Peruvian household recipe.[^v111-cauim-history][^v111-giroba]

See [fermented cassava as food and drink](../food/fermented-cassava-as-food-and-drink.md) and the [Shawi case](../culture/shawi-masato-and-water.md). The useful comparison is about materials, labor and how outsiders classify foods—not an unsupported assertion of common historical descent.
