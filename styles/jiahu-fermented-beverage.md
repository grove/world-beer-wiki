# Jiahu mixed fermented-beverage evidence

## The pottery, not the label, is the starting point

Chemical residue analysis published in 2004 interpreted Jiahu ceramic vessels as evidence of a mixed fermented beverage involving rice, honey and fruit. The assemblage dates to the Neolithic, and the argument comes from absorbed residue chemistry combined with nearby plant and artifact evidence. The authors did not recover a complete drink, observe a fermentation, or publish an exact recipe that can be reproduced with certainty.[^v113-jiahu-2004]

## A second analytical method changed the questions

A 2026 re-examination used starch and other microfossils from pottery residues, finding evidence the team interprets as cereal processing, Monascus-related fungi, enzyme-mediated starch change and more than one possible saccharification pathway. It proposes an important role for qu-like mold-assisted conversion and some malt-like signatures. The newer work does not transform the older chemical interpretation into a literal ingredient list or identify a modern yeast starter by name.[^v113-jiahu-2026]

## Treat an archaeological beverage as archaeological evidence

A responsible comparison records what was measured, how the ingredients were inferred, which claims depend on present-day analogies, and where the studies disagree. The material is valuable alongside Mijiaya and Shangshan evidence, but similarity in starch or fungal remains cannot prove that named present-day Asian beers descended directly from Jiahu brewing. “Beer” here is a useful research conversation, not a universal category imposed on ancient people.[^v113-jiahu-2004][^v113-jiahu-2026]
