# Cusco chicherías, maize harvests and women’s work

Felipe Vargas Faulbaum's 2019 field narrative places *chicha de jora* or *aqha* within maize harvesting, household labor and the social space of the *chichería*. In Huarocondo, the author describes a red marker outside a house selling chicha, and nearby workers negotiating the movement of harvested maize. Both scenes matter to the drink's public life.[^v110-cusco]

## Not just an ingredient list

The field account connects women's *chichera* labor, the preparation and sale of maize drink, family kitchens, customers and Quechua-language music. Brewing is therefore a part of a small economic and social system, not an isolated artisan technique. The ethnographer also studies changes associated with wider markets and modernity; the observed practice should not be presented as an unchanged survival from a pre-colonial past.[^v110-cusco]

## One place can differ from another

This Cusco-region account should be compared carefully with documented Piura household sales and archaeological interpretations from Moquegua. Peru includes many chicha practices, including variations in inputs, service, labor and context. The sources do not establish a single timetable for all maize-based fermentation, and a shared drink name does not prove institutional identity.[^v110-cusco]
