# Who gets to call a brewing number authoritative?

## Measurement has social uses

A published gravity, colour or bitterness value can appear neutral because it is expressed as a number. But deciding what to measure, why, and how to compare it is also an institutional choice. Historian James Sumner's study of John Richardson shows how an extract quantity and a saccharometer became persuasive in a particular eighteenth-century commercial brewing discussion. Its published abstract examines the construction and authority of that quantity, not its universal necessity for brewing worldwide.[^v116-sumner]

The Science Museum Group's catalogued c. 1830 saccharometer shows one example of a later manufactured measuring object. The surviving apparatus cannot tell us what every household brewer, apprentice or community knew or valued in that period.[^v116-museum]

## Rules depend on the institution

Modern BJCP judging education describes a framework for assigning sensory scores in competitions. Such a score can guide a judging decision but is not a community mandate, a public-health conclusion or a measurement of a drink's historical authenticity.[^v116-bjcpEval]

These distinctions are important when a formal laboratory analyzes a drink with a locally transmitted name. A microbiological profile, gravity reading or tasting panel should not automatically supersede a maker's account of the beverage, or convert an editorial comparison into proof of cultural descent.

## More responsible attribution

Ask whose claim is being reported: a researcher, instrument maker, regulatory body, judge, merchant or member of the brewing community. Preserve periods, sampling methods, dialects and sources. For living practices, voluntary local review remains needed before asserting shared identity or protected knowledge.
