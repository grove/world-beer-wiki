# Hop farms, drying work and the Kent landscape

A hop cone in a beer glass has already travelled through an agricultural landscape. Historic England's synthesis of oast and kiln studies shows why drying facilities were placed close to hop fields: freshly picked, moisture-rich hops deteriorated quickly. Harvest work therefore had to connect picking, transport, drying, cooling and packing over a compressed seasonal interval.[^v114-oasts]

The source traces hop-drying buildings across several English counties and a gradual concentration in Kent and East Sussex, Hampshire and Surrey, and Herefordshire and Worcestershire. It notes the different names *oast* and *hop kiln*. The regional architectural vocabulary matters: the conical cowl photographed on a Kent oast is not a universal form for every historical hop-drying building.[^v114-oasts]

Accounts of brewing often leap directly from a herb's flavor to a recipe. A labor-history account asks who coordinated field and kiln, how quickly a crop had to move, and what investment in buildings and fuel made storage possible. Historic England's article identifies multiple decades of building surveys and regional scholarship but is not a census of individual pickers or their wages. It cannot establish the experience of every seasonal worker.[^v114-oasts]

Follow the [harvest timing](../calendar/english-hop-harvest-and-drying-season.md), the [oast building](../objects/english-hop-oast.md), and [hop chemistry](../ingredients/hops.md). They describe three different layers: local work, physical infrastructure and chemical contribution to beer. None by itself tells us exactly what a finished nineteenth-century pint tasted like.[^v114-oasts]
