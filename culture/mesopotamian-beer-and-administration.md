# Mesopotamian beer as an administered resource

Ancient Mesopotamian beer appears in administrative tablets as an item to be received, issued and valued. A Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative record describes a Ur III messenger ration list containing bread, fish, oil, onions and **two named beer forms**. The named rations were associated with specific envoys and service, not automatically a daily beer wage for every worker.[^v19-cdli-ration]

## Documents reveal transactions more clearly than taste

Peter Damerow’s scholarly study explains why hundreds of brewing-related administrative texts are invaluable yet limited. They document quantities, commodities and disbursements because that is what officials needed to know; they rarely spell out the practical activities of grain preparation and fermentation. It is a category error to translate an accounting term directly into today’s “malt”, “wort” or “lager” without an argument for the equivalence.[^v19-damerow]

## What the evidence does not say

The ration tablet does not describe the flavor, alcohol content or brewing temperature of its named beers. It is not proof that every person in ancient Sumer received a daily allowance. Nor does the apparent distinction between categories prove a modern style family. This is administrative and linguistic evidence with a powerful social dimension, not a complete production manual.[^v19-cdli-ration][^v19-damerow]
