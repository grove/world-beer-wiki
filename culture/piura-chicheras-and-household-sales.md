# Chicheras, household production and sale days in Piura

The researcher Frances Hayashida interviewed women from ten *chicherías*, or household brewing businesses, around Chulucanas in the Piura region of northern Peru. The point of the visits was to understand brewing’s material and labor requirements, not to manufacture a universal Andean recipe. The account records women as named economic actors with distinct production schedules.[^v17-piura]

## Different households, different days

One family’s chicha might be offered on Tuesdays and Wednesdays, while another’s might be available at weekends. A white flag on a high pole signaled service in the observed setting. Because producers followed schedules that their neighbors knew, beer was also part of a local information system: people could choose a household by day, place and social connection.[^v17-piura]

## Raw materials reveal work networks

The Piura description mentions ground sprouted maize—*pachucho*—and ordinary maize flour, with material often purchased from mills rather than prepared entirely inside the household. The survey also discussed substantial differences from smaller or less permanent brewing operations in Lambayeque. That matters for archaeology: a hearth, milling tool and fermentation area may indicate different organizational scales, not separate civilizations.[^v17-piura]

## Whose voices and what limits?

This is a modern field sample around Chulucanas; the interviewees welcomed a visiting researcher into their homes. The published excerpt summarizes those visits but is not a complete first-person account of every brewer’s perspective. The study explicitly warns that contemporary practice provides comparisons for archaeology, not proof that a Chimu-era brewer used a particular twentieth-century technique.
