# Sake-making, koji and intangible heritage in Japan

UNESCO inscribed the traditional knowledge and skills of sake-making with koji mold in Japan in **2024**. The listing recognizes know-how and its social transmission, not simply a finished beverage name. In the described practice, workers manage temperature and humidity to enable koji growth and starch conversion; a lead brewer, the *tōji*, directs other workers, or *kurabito*.[^v17-unesco-sake]

## Why fermentation is also social knowledge

Koji’s starch-transforming role is technical, but successfully coordinating it depends on skilled judgment, tools, apprenticeship and collaboration. UNESCO’s description connects the craft to festivals, weddings, rites of passage and surrounding farming communities. It also describes how gender participation and professional organization have changed historically. No single modern workplace represents every brewery or era.[^v17-unesco-sake]

## A valuable comparison outside the beer label

The wiki includes sake as a **related rice ferment**, while beer styles and beer-adjacent beverages have distinct regional definitions. A common biochemical problem—making grain starch accessible to fermenting organisms—does not imply that Japanese sake should be judged as a lager or that every Asian dry starter works like cultivated koji.[^v17-unesco-sake]

## Limits of a UNESCO designation

Inscription establishes that a practice was accepted onto a specific heritage list and describes how the submitting parties presented it. It does not prove an uninterrupted ancient recipe, certify every commercial bottle or confer universal ownership of yeast. The UNESCO listing page may require a challenge in automated browsers, so this entry uses the indexed nomination description and decision without claiming to inspect every supporting dossier.
