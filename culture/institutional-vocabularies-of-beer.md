# Who gets to define beer? Institutions and situated vocabularies

## A name has a speaker

“Beer” is not only a recipe word. It may appear in law, a judging description, an advertisement, a household account or a research translation. Each speaker is doing different work. US tax administration and US federal label regulation themselves use two distinct beverage definitions, a good demonstration that one state has more than one administrative vocabulary.[^v115-defs]

BJCP's introduction presents competition groups as practical judging groupings and warns that neither historical origin nor all commercial examples can be read off those groups. That is an institutional purpose rather than a mandate to rename local brewing practices.[^v115-bjcp]

## How archives change the question

A 1991 ATF circular addresses the marketing of non-alcoholic malt beverages to industry actors. It establishes what an agency communicated on that date, but does not report how a particular household or pub understood its beverages. Using it to define a community's tradition would erase the gap between a regulatory institution and living speakers.[^v115-circular]

The editorial rule is therefore to **attribute the naming authority**. When a profile says a drink is a “style,” specify whether this derives from a judging edition, a legal market class, a cited maker description or a community account. For community-specific terminology, seek permission and local-language review rather than assigning regulatory synonyms across borders.
