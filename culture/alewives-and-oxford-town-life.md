# Oxford alewives: gender, trade and town regulation

A town-specific interpretation of Oxford commercial brewing in the fourteenth to sixteenth centuries.

## Why this is a town story

The Museum of Oxford describes a documented trade of local women selling ale, including the fourteenth-century brewer Maud London. Household-scale production could grow into a small business; not every maker was a widow or an occasional seller. Oxford’s political economy matters because an institutional customer—the university—also influenced brewing regulation.[^v15-oxford-alewives]

## When records create an illusion

Poll taxes and official records reveal particular brewers but can make larger, better documented businesses look like the whole trade. Undocumented occasional sellers are harder to count. That bias should be visible when the wiki presents names, numbers or sex ratios.[^v15-oxford-alewives]

## Regulation and protest

Historical scholarship shows that women could contest rules over ale prices and inspection. Bennett discusses an Exeter protest against price-setting in 1317; it is a comparator, not proof of an equivalent Oxford event. The common thread is the relationship between brewing livelihoods and local power.[^v15-bennett-laws]

## What this is not

This page does not claim that medieval ale was invariably weak, always drunk instead of water, or brewed only by women. Such broad assertions require separate evidence on strength, potable water, and demographics, not a single town museum narrative.[^v15-oxford-alewives]
