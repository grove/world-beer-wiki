# Bere barley and the Orkney revival: local farming is more than a grain label

## A landscape and a supply chain

An open review by Martin and colleagues describes the survival and revival of Bere, a six-row barley cultivated in some Scottish-island settings, after extensive replacement by modern cultivars elsewhere. Its account connects food products, grain milling, historical distilling and beer-related uses with crop conservation and market work. A present crop name, however, does not establish that every farm uses identical techniques or that every product is made from Bere alone.[^v117-bere-review]

The University of the Highlands and Islands describes research on growing Bere, trait selection and the development of local markets. This records institutional activities; the page was available through search indexing but did not consistently load in direct inspection. It is not first-person testimony from every Orkney farmer, miller or brewer.[^v117-bere-institute]

## Care with continuity

The Neolithic cultivation of barley on Orkney is a historical context, not proof that the named present-day landrace was the same plant in every ancient field. The review explicitly leaves the date of introduction uncertain. A claim that Bere has a long regional presence therefore needs a narrower wording than a claim of unbroken identical ancient brewing practice.

For the wiki, preserve local language, named institutions, crop context and distinctions among grain, flour, malt and drink. Future work should involve voluntary local historical and practitioner review, and avoid assigning protected knowledge to an academic paper.
