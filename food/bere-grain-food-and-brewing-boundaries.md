# Bere barley as bread grain, animal feed and brewing material

## A grain's uses are not interchangeable labels

The Bere literature describes its role in northern Scottish-island food and agricultural systems. Martin and colleagues summarize historic and modern uses including beremeal, animal feed, malt and distillation contexts. Those uses show the value of one crop across a food-and-drink supply chain, not the existence of one mandatory recipe or a claim that every named Bere product is a beer.[^v117-bere-review]

The University of the Highlands and Islands overview describes cultivar research and local market development. This helps explain why a contemporary heritage grain can support milling and other producers, while remaining an institutional description rather than personal testimony from all Orkney households.[^v117-bere-institute]

## Classify the actual product

Barley flour in a bannock, a malt made from Bere and a fermented beer using Bere are different materials. An ingredient association cannot infer a finished product's ABV, allergen status, sweetness or traditional identity. Malt kilning, wort composition, fermentation and presentation also change what reaches the table.

A food history needs both material evidence and situated cultural context. Modern branding and historical crop continuity are separate claims; the presence of Bere in a recipe today does not demonstrate an identical ancient formulation.

A pairing preference is another question entirely, and the existing [beer and food research](beer-food-pairing-as-a-hypothesis.md) describes why it requires its own study design. Compare [Bere as an ingredient](../ingredients/bere-six-row-barley.md) and [the Orkney crop revival](../culture/orkney-bere-knowledge-and-revival.md) rather than treating the entire category as one dish or drink.
