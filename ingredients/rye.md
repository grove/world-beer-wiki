# Rye

Rye is a brewing cereal with both flavour and process consequences. Its name identifies the grain, while “rye malt” additionally identifies malting. The distinction matters because a product's processing and share of the grist affect its contribution; “made with rye” is not a complete style description.[^ing-crisp-rye]

## Character is broader than “spicy”

Crisp describes its rye malt with caramel-like notes at lower inclusion and a spicier finish at higher inclusion. Weyermann's pale rye malt is described in more bread-and-honey terms. These producer descriptions are useful precisely because they do not reduce all rye to one sensation. Their products and sensory frames differ; neither description should be imposed on every finished rye beer.[^ing-crisp-rye][^ing-weyermann-rye]

## Brewing implications

Rye is huskless, and the BJCP Roggenbier account discusses its tendency to produce a gummy mash. Crisp also flags beta-glucan-related run-off issues and suggests a filtration aid. This is a process distinction, not a defect inherent in the taste of rye beer.[^bjcp-2021-27h][^ing-crisp-rye]

[Rice hulls](rice.md) illustrate the difference between ingredient function and fermentable grain: a brewer can use a material derived from rice to help separation without making a rice-flavoured or rice-fermented beer. Record whether a source means an extract-bearing grain or a physical filter aid.[^ing-briess-products]

## Rye, clove and caraway are different claims

The modern BJCP description of [Roggenbier](../styles/roggenbier.md) combines rye character with a wheat-beer yeast profile. It specifically rejects treating caraway as the source of the style's rye spiciness. This is a useful caution for reading tasting notes: bread associations can be evocative without establishing that every ingredient in the corresponding bread was used in the beer.[^bjcp-2021-27h]

Compare Roggenbier with [Rye IPA](../styles/rye-ipa.md), and use [Kvass](../styles/kvass.md) as a separate route into bread-associated fermentation. A shared grain or descriptive word is not evidence that these drinks share one process or lineage.

## Evidence note

The two maltster accounts concern distinct present-day products. The Roggenbier reference is a modern judging definition. Historical rye use and regional farmhouse variations require additional local sources; no fixed rye percentage is asserted here.
