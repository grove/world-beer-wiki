# Cassava

Cassava is a root crop, not a cereal malt. It is a substantial food and beverage substrate in the Guyanese community account published by Cobra Collective, where cassava products and the knowledge needed to prepare them are part of everyday life. A cassava-based drink should therefore be described through its own processing tradition rather than treated as barley beer with a grain replacement.[^parakari-community]

## Starch conversion is a separate question

Henkel's study of [Parakari](../styles/parakari.md) in Aishalton, Guyana, documents an amylolytic *Rhizopus* mould stage followed by alcoholic fermentation. This is direct evidence that mould-assisted saccharification is not exclusively an Asian brewing phenomenon. It is evidence for that documented process, not proof that every cassava ferment uses the same mould or technique.[^parakari-study]

The distinction between starch conversion and alcohol production is useful across the wiki. Identify what prepares or breaks down the starch before deciding what “fermented cassava” means. The ingredient name alone does not establish the source of enzymes or the organisms responsible for fermentation.

## Mixtures matter

Miguel and colleagues describe a [Caxiri](../styles/caxiri.md) preparation containing cassava, maize and sweet potato. Its microbiological account identifies both yeasts and bacteria. Calling the studied drink simply “cassava beer” can hide those additional substrates and the mixed fermentation. The study's measured concentrations are batch observations, not mandatory specifications for every caxiri.[^caxiri-study]

For another distinct comparison, consult [Masato de yuca](../styles/masato-de-yuca.md). Do not assume from a shared root crop that masato, caxiri and parakari are interchangeable names or follow one conversion method.

## Processing knowledge belongs in the description

The Parikwarinawa community account stresses proper preparation of bitter cassava and the transmission of practical knowledge between generations. It also identifies the cyanide hazard of consuming inadequately processed bitter cassava. These details are part of the material's identity, not a reason to replace community practice with an improvised brewing recipe.[^parakari-community]

## Evidence note

The parakari and caxiri papers were used at abstract level for the claims made here; the community source's written account was consulted, not its video transcribed. No general fermentation schedule, safety guarantee, sensory profile or claim of common ancestry is inferred. ## Edition 1.11: newly sourced connections

Cassava appears in several geographically and culturally distinct fermented foods. A historical interpretation of Guarani and Tupinambá cauim includes equipment and ingredients from colonial-era records; a later Tapirapé microbiology study followed a cassava ferment with changing acidity. Both broaden the botanical reference but are **not** the same recipe, era or community.[^v111-cauim-history][^v111-cauim-bacteria]

Browse the [cauim article](../styles/cauim.md), [Tapirapé food context](../culture/tapirape-cauim-and-staple-food.md) and [giroba ethnography](../styles/giroba.md). Neither microbial acidification nor the word 'cassava' alone establishes nutritional adequacy, safe toxin removal or a universal preparation method; those are separate questions requiring tested procedures and appropriate expertise.
