# Mould and mixed starters

Some brewing systems obtain starch-degrading enzymes from mould-grown material or mixed starters rather than relying entirely on malt. The ingredient record should distinguish the substrate carrying a culture, the organisms present and the enzymes or fermentation they supply. “Yeast cake” can obscure that complexity.[^ing-jssa-process][^ing-nuruk-communities]

## Koji and nuruk are distinct examples

For sake, the Japan Sake and Shochu Makers Association describes koji as steamed rice colonized by *Aspergillus oryzae*. The resulting enzymes participate in starch conversion while yeast produces alcohol in the mash. Koji, the mould and the alcoholic yeast are therefore connected but separate concepts.[^ing-jssa-process]

Nuruk is a Korean fermentation starter with a microbial community. A 2022 study followed changes in fungi, bacteria and enzyme activity as nuruk matured. A separate investigation of traditional and commercial wheat-based nuruk also found variation in bacterial composition. Neither supports assigning one fixed species list to all nuruk.[^ing-nuruk-communities][^ing-nuruk-bacteria]

## A starter comparison is not a cultural classification

An experimental rice-wine study compared selected *Monascus* koji and cultured rice-nuruk preparations. Fermentation yields, acids and volatile compounds differed under its conditions. The materials were deliberately prepared for that trial; its “nuruk” treatment cannot be assumed to represent every spontaneously developed traditional nuruk.[^ing-rice-starter-comparison]

This is why the same English word “starter” is not enough to equate products. Record at least the local name, grain or other carrier, whether organisms were selected or maintained as a community, and the process in which the starter is used.

## A useful comparison beyond Asia

Henkel's [Parakari](../styles/parakari.md) study documents a starch-degrading *Rhizopus* stage in a Guyanese cassava fermentation. It provides a cross-regional process comparison, without establishing that parakari derives from Asian starter traditions.[^parakari-study]

Explore [Sake](../styles/sake.md), [Makgeolli](../styles/makgeolli.md), [Haria](../styles/haria.md), [Kodo ko jaanr](../styles/kodo-ko-jaanr.md), [Bhaati jaanr](../styles/bhaati-jaanr.md), [Sato](../styles/sato.md) and [Tuak](../styles/tuak-rice.md) as separate named traditions. Their local starter vocabularies and practices should be retained where documented, not standardized into “koji” for convenience.

## Evidence note

The sources cover defined producers and studies. They do not justify assigning organisms by appearance, identifying every rice ferment as beer, or treating all mould-grown material as a suitable brewing ingredient. The comparison concerns biological roles, not interchangeability.
