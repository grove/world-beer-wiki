# Barley and malt

Barley is a cereal; malt is grain that has been germinated and dried under controlled conditions. The terms are related but not interchangeable. Wheat and rye can also be malted. In barley brewing, malting prepares the grain for subsequent extraction and starch conversion rather than simply turning the entire kernel into sugar.[^ing-briess-malting]

## What malting changes

Steeping hydrates grain, germination develops or activates enzymes and modifies the structures around its starch reserves, and drying stops growth. Base malt retains useful enzyme activity. More intense heating develops different colours and aromas while reducing that activity. This explains why two products made from the same cereal can perform very differently in a mash.[^ing-briess-malting]

| Material or treatment | Distinction to record |
| --- | --- |
| Base malt | A major source of extract and, depending on the product, starch-converting enzymes. |
| More intensely kilned malt | Heat develops flavour and colour; check the actual product rather than assuming all such malts are enzymatically identical. |
| Caramel or crystal malt | Starch is converted inside moist kernels; later heating develops caramel colour and flavour. |
| Roasted malt or grain | “Roasted” describes a treatment; it does not by itself establish whether the starting grain was malted. |

Briess describes distinct kiln, roasting and combined processes, and evaluates colour, enzyme activity and wort flavour separately. A dark colour measurement therefore cannot substitute for a sensory description or a specification of the ingredient used.[^ing-briess-specialty]

## Smoke is another dimension

Smoking and roasting should be recorded separately. Weyermann's oak-smoked wheat malt is a pale, enzymatically active product with smoke character; it demonstrates that smoke need not imply dark colour or barley. Follow [Wheat](wheat.md), [Rauchbier](../styles/rauchbier.md) and [Grodziskie](../styles/grodziskie.md) to compare different uses of this distinction.[^ing-weyermann-smoked-wheat]

## Reading a malt-led style

A description such as “bready”, “caramelly” or “roasty” is an impression, not a complete ingredient list. For research, ask which grain, which treatment and which proportion the source actually identifies. Also distinguish malt flavour from sweetness left after fermentation; [Sugars](sugars.md) and [Brewing yeast](brewing-yeast.md) explain that second question.

Useful comparisons are [Munich Helles](../styles/munich-helles.md), [Munich Dunkel](../styles/munich-dunkel.md), [Märzen](../styles/marzen.md), [English Porter](../styles/english-porter.md) and [Irish Stout](../styles/irish-stout.md). These links are a reading route, not a claim that a modern malt brand reproduces every historical beer.

## Evidence note

The technical accounts describe current malt production. Their precise times, temperatures and usage recommendations are not universal rules for all maltsters or traditional home malting. Historical malt requires its own dated evidence.
