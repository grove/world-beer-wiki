# Hop bitter fractions and what a single analytical signal cannot identify

## A hop contains more than one source of bitterness

During brewing, hop alpha acids can be transformed into iso-alpha acids. Other components, including oxidation products and polyphenols, can also contribute to a bitter impression or be present in measured extracts. A published review by Klimczak and Cioch-Skoneczny describes these families and notes the difficulty of translating their combined chemistry into a single sensory expectation.[^v116-bitterReview]

The ASBC's public Beer 23 method summary explains that one conventional bitterness assay extracts a mixture of bittering compounds and measures its light absorption at 275 nanometres. It is not the same operation as separating and individually quantifying each chemical species. Results can be valuable for within-process comparisons without identifying which fraction created the detected signal.[^v116-bitterMethod]

A publisher abstract from a controlled wort-and-beer study further demonstrates why closely matched measured bitterness units need not imply the same iso-alpha acid concentrations or perceived intensities. Its particular experimental beers are examples, not default values for every style.[^v116-ibuStudy]

## Better descriptions than “more hops equals more bitter”

Ingredient identity, crop condition, form and point of addition affect the chemical mixture that later reaches a beer. Distinguish an added dose of hops, a measured IBU, a chromatographic compound profile and a sensory bitter score. These are not interchangeable.

Consult [hop alpha acids](hop-alpha-acids.md), [the IBU assay guide](../processes/bitterness-units-and-hop-chemistry.md) and [the established sensory caveat](../sensory/bitterness-is-not-one-number.md). This is a research interpretation, not a prescribed hopping schedule or a safety claim.
