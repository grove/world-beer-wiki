# Dissolved carbon dioxide: gas, ingredient and serving condition

## Carbonation is not just visible foam

Carbon dioxide can be created during fermentation, retained under pressure and released as a beer is opened and served. The distinction between gas **dissolved in beer** and bubbles already visible in a glass matters: a glass with few rising bubbles can still contain dissolved CO2, and the rate of release depends on how the liquid is disturbed and presented.

Liger-Belair and Cilindre examined a bottled commercial lager and modeled CO2 bubble nucleation after pouring. Their work connects dissolved concentration, temperature, pressure and microscopic surface pockets. They did **not** provide a fixed bubble number for every beer, nor a universal target for traditional, low-alcohol or nitrogen-assisted products.[^v115-bubbles]

## A change of setting

Inside a sealed package, headspace conditions help retain gas. Pouring changes pressure and provides surfaces where bubble formation can start. Once served, the same product's bubbling will vary with the glass, time and liquid condition. Thus the measured package concentration is not identical to an observation of foam at the table.

The Brewers Association's draught quality resource also treats dispense gas and balanced equipment as distinct quality questions. That technical layer should not be folded into a recipe or an ingredient identity simply because both involve gas.[^v115-ba]

## Edition 1.19: newly sourced connections

Dissolved CO₂ in a beer, fermenter off-gas and carbon accounted in a company footprint are different observations. Grand and colleagues' 2025 study evaluates potential recovery of fermentation CO₂ for craft brewers, with findings from five French interviews; the work does not measure the dissolved carbonation of every final beer or certify recovered gas quality.[^v119-craftco2]

The GHG Protocol's direct, purchased-energy and value-chain emissions definitions clarify why gas capture or reuse cannot itself establish a whole-product net-zero claim. In particular, a beverage's CO₂ bubbles are not a carbon-storage credit simply because they came from fermentation.[^v119-ghgfaq]
