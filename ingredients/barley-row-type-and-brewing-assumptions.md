# Two-row and six-row barley: a trait is not an origin story

Brewing references often distinguish two-row and six-row barley for practical reasons. These labels describe grain-ear morphology; they do not reliably specify a beer’s flavor, a brewery’s exact malt recipe or the chronological ancestry of a locally harvested grain. The Qasr Ibrim archaeogenetic study provides an unusual example where ancient barley of two-row appearance carried a mutation associated with a six-row ancestor.[^v19-nubian-barley]

## Visible form and genetic history

The researchers analyzed ancient material and the *vrs1* locus to investigate the row trait. Their interpretation of local adaptation is tied to that dataset and its preservation conditions. It does not demonstrate that a barley row type produces a particular lager, that ancient Nubians used the grains in a documented beer, or that a present-day malt variety is genetically identical to an archaeological sample.[^v19-nubian-barley]

## What a useful recipe or archaeology record needs

For present brewing, record the actual malt specification and process rather than assuming one row type predicts the final profile. For excavated grains, record archaeobotanical context, chronology, identification and analytical limitations. Evidence for fermentation belongs to a separate chain that might involve residues, vessels or records. Do not treat a genetic paper as a sensory panel.[^v19-nubian-barley]
