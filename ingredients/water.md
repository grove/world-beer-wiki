# Water

Water is both a raw material and the medium in which mashing, fermentation and flavour expression take place. For understanding a beer, its mineral composition and interaction with the grist matter more than the pH number on a bottle of drinking water. The relevant pH changes as malt and other ingredients enter the system.[^ing-water-aha]

## Three measurements that should not be confused

| Measurement | What it describes | Why a beer reader should care |
| --- | --- | --- |
| pH | Acidity of the sample being measured | Source-water pH, mash pH and finished-beer pH are different observations. |
| Alkalinity | Capacity to neutralize added acid | Water can resist the acidity introduced by the grist. |
| Hardness | Principally calcium and magnesium content | “Hard” does not tell the reader every relevant ion concentration. |

These are related quantities, not synonyms. The BJCP study guide explains their interaction through dissolved ions and malt buffering; it does not justify identifying a beer from a “hard” or “soft” label alone.[^ing-water-bjcp]

## Effects in brewing and in the glass

Calcium and magnesium take part in mash chemistry, while sulfate, chloride and sodium can affect flavour perception. Sulfate can emphasize a dry, bitter impression; chloride can support a fuller impression. These are influences within a finished beer, not switches that create an IPA or a stout. The concentrations, base beer and other ingredients remain relevant.[^ing-water-aha]

Palmer's technical presentation treats water adjustment as two linked tasks: reaching a suitable mash environment and supporting the desired flavour. It describes salt additions, acid additions and ion removal, and asks brewers to consider changes in the water source over the year. Consequently, a location name alone is insufficient evidence of the water that actually reached a brewery's mash.[^ing-palmer-water]

## Historical and stylistic interpretation

An ingredient account should distinguish source water, treated brewing liquor and finished beer. A modern brewer can adjust water; the fact that two beers are made in different cities does not establish that their brewing water differs in the same way as two untreated municipal samples. Equally, recreating a published city profile is not proof of recreating the historical beer.

For comparisons, read [Czech Premium Pale Lager](../styles/czech-premium-pale-lager.md), [English IPA](../styles/english-ipa.md) and [Irish Stout](../styles/irish-stout.md) alongside their other ingredients. Treat geographical water stories as questions requiring dated brewery evidence.

## Evidence note

These sources explain brewing chemistry and contemporary practice. This page deliberately avoids universal ion targets, fixed sulfate-to-chloride prescriptions and unsupported claims that geology single-handedly created a style.
