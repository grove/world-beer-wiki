# Smoked malt and the point where smoke enters beer

In Schlenkerla's account of its own production, malt acquires smoke character through exposure to smoke from burning beechwood logs. The brewery identifies its malt preparation as the source of the smoky contribution; the explanation does not require adding a smoky food to the finished beer.[^atlas-schlenkerla-smoke]

## Place the treatment in the sequence

The useful distinction is **when and to what material the treatment happens**. Smoking grain during malt preparation differs from roasting grain for a dark flavor, baking a mash, or maturing finished beer in contact with wood. The existing specialty guide keeps these operations separate.[^atlas-smoke-context]

Record grain identity, smoking material where known, the malt's source, its role in the grist and the underlying beer. “Smoked” alone cannot determine color, fermentation method, strength or whether the whole malt bill received the same treatment.

## Compare traditions rather than erase them

Read [Rauchbier](../styles/rauchbier.md), [Grodziskie](../styles/grodziskie.md) and [Stjørdalsøl](../styles/stjordalsol.md) through their individual sources. The shared word “smoke” creates a good reading route, not a claim that the traditions use the same grain, wood or production institution.

The [sensory comparison](../sensory/smoke-roast-and-wood.md) adds another warning: a dark appearance or a coffee-like aroma cannot identify a smoke-malting operation on its own.

## Evidence note

The Schlenkerla source is primary evidence of the producer's stated method, not independent proof of its marketing claims about exclusivity or worldwide historical continuity. This reference supplies no interchangeable list of woods or burning materials and no instruction to use unidentified or treated wood in food production.
