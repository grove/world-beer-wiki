# English hop harvest: the field-to-kiln season

Agricultural calendars do not all run on a fixed public holiday. Hops have to be harvested at a suitable stage and then handled quickly, and the moment differs with weather and place. Historic England explains why drying buildings stood near hop fields: moist cones could spoil before they reached the kiln. The calendar relevant to the worker was thus a sequence of ripening, picking and rapid processing.[^v114-oasts]

The article describes a drying period on a kiln floor, followed by cooling in the stowage building and pressing into large pockets for shipment. Those stages connect the crop calendar to fuel, transport, storage and brewery supply. They explain why seasonality shaped an entire rural landscape rather than merely the date a brewer bought a bag of hops.[^v114-oasts]

Regional names and infrastructure also matter. A Kentish oast and a kiln in Herefordshire may solve the same handling problem but have different forms; a former oast converted to a residence does not prove it is currently drying hops. Historic England identifies shifts in English growing regions over centuries, so a static map of 1900 cannot be used as today's harvest forecast.[^v114-oasts]
