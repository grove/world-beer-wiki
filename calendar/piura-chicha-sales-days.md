# Household chicha sales days in the Piura field study

A historical or cultural calendar needs more than official holidays. In the Piura households visited by Frances Hayashida, commercial chicha was made and sold on locally known days of the week. The published study gives examples of businesses serving Tuesday–Wednesday or Saturday–Sunday. These are **observed household service patterns**, not a universal Peruvian drinking calendar.[^v17-piura]

## A weekly rhythm and a visible cue

A white flag on a tall pole could mark a chichería offering beer. For the fieldworker, these temporal and material signs helped identify production locations and understand how drinkers accessed them. For historians, they demonstrate how a community’s relationship to brewing can be organized through ordinary market rhythms rather than only monumental feasts.[^v17-piura]

## Do not extrapolate a future timetable

The observed businesses and their sales days belonged to the study’s fieldwork, not to a verified present-day venue directory. Naming a producer in an archaeological article is not permission to show an unconfirmed contemporary opening schedule or invite tourists to an address. This atlas therefore stores timing as a locally described practice with no date arithmetic or future edition generated.

## Agricultural and ritual time are separate

This weekly rhythm sits beside larger Andean traditions of communal labor and ritual drinking. Those subjects may interact, but a Tuesday sales sign does not document the day of a historical harvest ceremony. Compare the chapter on chicha and reciprocity for evidence from a different time and kind of source.
