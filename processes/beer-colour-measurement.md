# What is actually measured when a laboratory reports beer colour?

## State the sample before stating the number

A colour result may refer to sweet wort, fermented beer, a filtered sample or a naturally hazy pour. These are not identical materials. Thesseling and colleagues' laboratory guide describes a spectrophotometric method for measuring wort or beer at 430 nanometres and relates EBC and SRM reporting. That is a method applied under specified sample preparation, not a visual image-to-style classifier.[^v116-lab]

The BJCP's colour guide explains the SRM relation to light absorbance and an approximate conversion to EBC. It also shows a printed visual comparison guide, which has a different purpose from laboratory instrumentation. Printing, glass thickness and lighting can make a visual estimate diverge from an analytical reading.[^v116-color]

## A dark or cloudy sample needs context

Optical absorbance depends on the preparation and light path. Suspended particles can scatter light, so attributing an unprepared sample's reading purely to dissolved colour compounds is hazardous. Record whether the sample was degassed, clarified or diluted, and identify the reported method rather than silently converting an observation into an exact figure.

A single 430-nanometre number is also narrower than the full appearance of a beer: red and brown hue, haze, foam and illuminated glass conditions are not automatically measured by that result. Two beers may share an SRM estimate yet look unlike one another.

A useful wiki entry records the measured matrix, method, unit, significant figures and measurement status.
