# How oxygen enters and remains in packaged beer

Beer after fermentation can pick up oxygen during transfers, filtration and filling. A package can hold oxygen dissolved in the liquid and in the headspace; later ingress may occur through certain closures or materials. A 2022 review distinguishes these pathways and emphasizes why package oxygen cannot be inferred from the word *can* or *bottle* alone.[^v111-metals]

## Different times, different chemistry

Oxygen exposure before fermentation and exposure after packaging are not interchangeable. Yeast may use oxygen for biomass production during particular fermentation phases, whereas unwanted pickup into finished beer can accelerate oxidative aroma changes. Metal ions and beer constituents mediate the reactions, so a simple volume of oxygen does not dictate one flavor outcome or expiry date.[^v111-metals]

## Packaging comparison needs two boundaries

Chemical protection concerns light transmission, oxygen ingress and closure behavior. An environmental footprint concerns material production, transport, recycling and the actual distribution system. A can may protect beer against light but that alone does not prove the can's whole-life emissions are lower than reusable glass under all assumptions. Keep the analytical question visible before recommending one package type.[^v111-metals]

## Edition 1.18: newly sourced connections

A dissolved-oxygen result taken from a tank is not equal to total oxygen in a sealed package. The indexed Analytica EBC 11.5.1 method description addresses the dissolved liquid and gas headspace phases of beer in bottles, cans, kegs and casks.[^v118-ebc-tpo] The Brewers Association's 2024 freshness-series landing page identifies monitoring and sensor methods across brewing, cellar and packaging. The full member-only material was not accessible; therefore its public introduction is not a universal numerical specification for any brewery.[^v118-ba-fresh24]
