# Bitterness units are a method result, not a taste thermometer

## What a laboratory method reports

The ASBC's Beer 23 public summary describes an analytical determination using extraction and an absorbance reading at 275 nanometres. The result is useful for comparing wort or beer samples and monitoring process consistency. It is **not** a direct measurement of a drinker's subjective bitterness intensity, and the complete proprietary method was not independently read for this edition.[^v116-bitterMethod]

A 2023 chemistry review explains why other hop compounds, oxidation products and polyphenols complicate a direct mapping between spectrophotometric bittering units and specific iso-alpha acid content. Interpreting dry-hopped or aged-hop beers can require more selective chemical analysis and a sensory experiment rather than a single number.[^v116-bitterReview]

An abstracted ASBC journal study compared brews with similar measured bitterness values but strikingly different iso-alpha acid profiles. The authors' particular sample settings do not establish a fixed correction factor for every beer, but their contrast warns against treating two equal IBUs as chemically identical.[^v116-ibuStudy]

## Four kinds of evidence

A recipe calculator predicts bitterness from assumptions about hopping and utilisation. A lab assay reports a specified chemical signal. A compound-specific method can distinguish selected molecular classes. A sensory panel studies perception. These outputs have different uncertainty and cannot replace each other.

For research claims, record the brewing stage, sample treatment, whether an IBU was measured or predicted, analytical method and any sensory conditions. The chemicals involved in the actual assay can be hazardous, so this guide is conceptual, not an instruction for informal solvent handling.
