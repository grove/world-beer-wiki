# How beer foam forms and why it falls

Foam requires gas to make bubbles, but it also requires surfaces that resist immediate collapse. In beer those surfaces contain proteins and other compounds that reach the glass after a long production chain. Studies of barley **Protein Z** and **LTP1** help explain why some malt-related proteins can survive brewing and contribute to stability.[^v18-foam-z]

## What changes between grain and glass

Protein extraction, modification, wort heating and fermentation alter the pool of foam-related molecules. Proteomics research has followed abundance of selected barley proteins during mash and boil; finding a protein in a sample does not establish that it directly caused a particular foam height. A separate 2025 heat study examined chemical changes in free and lipid-associated LTP1 and their relationship to experimentally measured foam quality.[^v18-foam-proteome][^v18-foam-ltp]

## Serve matters, but it is another experiment

Carbonation, vessel cleanliness, oil contamination and the act of pouring influence bubble creation and persistence. These need separate controlled observations; the cited molecular studies do not offer a universal glassware recommendation or explain every observed pour. The [Czech foam service](../objects/czech-foam-pours.md) article records a serving tradition, not proof of a singular foam chemistry.
