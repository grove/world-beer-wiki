# Baked mash in keptinis

What goes into the oven matters. In the keptinis process observed by Garshol, the Jančys family first prepared a mash from pale malt, then baked that mash before extracting wort. This is not simply the addition of baked bread to an otherwise ordinary beer.[^atlas-keptinis]

## Follow the material

The field account describes mash transferred into metal boxes, a dark crust developing during oven heating, and the baked material subsequently broken up and rinsed through a straw-containing filter. A dark beer therefore emerged from a pale-malt starting point through treatment of the mash itself.[^atlas-keptinis]

The metal boxes are especially instructive. The brewer described them as a practical innovation compared with older bread-like mash portions. Changing a container did not necessarily abandon the distinctive operation. This is a documented example of transmission and adaptation occurring together.[^atlas-keptinis]

## Three different comparisons

[Keptinis](../styles/keptinis.md) names the tradition in its geographical and family context. [Decoction](decoction.md) compares another way of applying additional heat during mashing. [Smoked malt](../ingredients/smoked-malt.md) moves the question to an earlier stage: how the grain was dried before brewing.

Ask whether color or aroma was developed in the malt, in a mash treatment, in the wort or during maturation. Those questions are more useful than treating all dark or toasted beers as products of one technique.

## Evidence note

The observer did not establish a dependable oven temperature, and this entry does not turn an estimate into a universal schedule. Nor does it reduce the chemistry to a claim that only one reaction can occur in each stage. The field account supports the observed sequence and tasting context, not an exhaustive chemical analysis or a recipe valid for every keptinis maker.
