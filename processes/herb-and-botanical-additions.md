# Botanical beer additions: historical evidence versus modern recipe

## A plant is not a procedure

Historical accounts of gruit list botanical materials including bog myrtle and yarrow; reference texts also discuss spruce and pine shoots in different brewing traditions. The presence of one plant name does not reveal whether material was boiled, infused, steeped or used in filtration in a particular site or period.[^v13-gruit][^v13-bog][^v13-spruce]

## Ingredient identity can be uncertain

Common names can name multiple species or preparations, and older terminology can refer to a tax right or supplied mixture rather than a particular herb. A reliable reconstruction requires a dated and located source for the specific material and operation. Modern botanical beers should record their own formula without backdating it into a universal medieval practice.[^v13-gruit]

## Three questions to record

What species and plant part? When and how was it applied? Which source actually attests that use? Read [Gruit family](../categories/herbal-beer-and-gruit.md), [Bog myrtle](../ingredients/bog-myrtle.md) and [Spruce tips](../ingredients/spruce-and-pine-shoots.md).

## Evidence boundaries

No dosage, safe plant identification, or preservation claims are supplied. Foraging and toxicity require specialist guidance. All dated observations are tied to the source and location described above. This is a research draft.
