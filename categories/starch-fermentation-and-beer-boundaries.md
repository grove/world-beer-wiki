# Starch fermentation and the boundaries of beer

This wiki includes some neighboring grain and starch ferments because they illuminate brewing. Inclusion does not assert that their makers call them beer, or that one English classification overrides a local identity. The file's `type` and `scope` are part of its meaning, not administrative decoration.

The most useful comparison begins with three questions: **what supplies the carbohydrate, what converts starch to fermentable sugars, and what carries out fermentation?** A grain name alone cannot answer all three.

## Two examples of why conversion matters

Imayotsukasa's account of sake explains how koji is cultivated on steamed rice as part of preparing the grain for fermentation. This differs from developing enzymes through grain germination. Neither comparison justifies replacing *sake* with a generic “rice lager” label.[^sake-producer]

The Munkoyo fermentation study describes a maize preparation using Rhynchosia root as an amylase source. That makes the roots' enzymatic role a useful comparison with malt or fungal starters, without making the drink a standardized alcoholic beer.[^trad-munkoyo-fermentation]

See [mould and mixed starters](../ingredients/mould-and-mixed-starters.md) for a deeper account. A starter can contain several kinds of organisms with different jobs. Treating the entire starter as “yeast” loses the distinction between starch breakdown, alcohol production and acid formation.

## Ingredient overlap is not cultural identity

A [Rice Lager](../styles/rice-lager.md) and a rice-based regional ferment share a substrate word while potentially differing in conversion, fermentation, service and cultural meaning. Similarly, [sorghum and millet](../ingredients/sorghum-and-millet.md) are useful ingredients to compare across regions, not a single pan-African or pan-Asian style.

Read [maize](../ingredients/maize.md), [rice](../ingredients/rice.md), [cassava](../ingredients/cassava.md), and [fruit](../ingredients/fruit.md) alongside the relevant local profile. Ingredient articles explain material roles; tradition profiles carry local evidence and naming.

## How to answer a boundary question

Prefer “this corpus includes it as a related fermented-grain tradition for comparison” when that is the page's type. Do not translate “related” into a claim about legal beverage classification, nutritional suitability or equivalence between national categories. If a user's question requires a community's own definition, seek that evidence rather than deciding from starch chemistry alone.
