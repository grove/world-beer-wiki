# Field fungi are not the same as brewing starter microbes

## The word fungus hides two different questions

Beer fermentations can deliberately use yeasts or complex starter communities. A plant-pathogenic fungus found on barley in the field, however, is not thereby the intended fermenter of the later beer. In field work from Manitoba, Jayathissa and colleagues deliberately compared Fusarium graminearum isolates on two barley cultivars before laboratory malting. Their fungal density and disease observations concern diseased crop material, not the composition of a living household starter or a recommended fermentation culture.[^v117-fungal-strains]

A 2022 review of Fusarium mycotoxins in malt and beer follows another axis: certain cereal-infecting fungi can produce chemical metabolites that may persist or change through processing. That is an ingredient-chain risk question. It does not demonstrate that a viable fungus survives to dominate a finished beer or that every grain-grown fungus makes the same compounds.[^v117-fusarium-review]

## Keep detection, function and practice apart

A fungal DNA measurement can indicate genetic material in a sample. An isolated organism in a lab can be identified under those test conditions. Detection of a metabolite is yet another result. None alone shows cultural ownership, a traditional recipe, metabolic function across all batches, or food safety. In particular, a well-intentioned description of “mixed fermentation” should not reclassify a crop disease as a maker-selected starter.

Record the species or assay, location, growth or DNA evidence, stage, sample size and the source's named community or crop trial.
