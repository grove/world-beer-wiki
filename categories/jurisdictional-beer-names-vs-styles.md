# A regulated beer name is not a judging style

## Three institutions, three questions

A label, a beer-competition entry and a community name can use identical words while answering different questions. The US Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau distinguishes **beer** under federal tax rules from **malt beverage** under its separate federal labeling framework. The latter requires malted barley and hops, while the former definition includes certain other fermentables. These are legal scopes, not a global verdict on whether a traditional drink belongs in an encyclopedia.[^v115-defs]

A TTB class designation describes a regulated product in a particular market. The agency's guidance treats certain class names differently below 0.5% alcohol by volume; a word on an American retail label is therefore not interchangeable with a fermentation style record.[^v115-clas]

The BJCP explicitly says its categories group styles for judging rather than ancestry or legislation. Its published categories can inform a sensory comparison, but they cannot override either product labeling law or the words used by a maker or community.[^v115-bjcp]

## A safer knowledge model

For a product or reference, record the **jurisdiction**, **legal category and label claim**, **declared strength**, **guideline/edition if used**, and **community or maker terminology** separately. A single untyped field called “style” destroys these distinctions. The same word may be historical, commercial, educational, or legally constrained depending on where and when it is used.

An entry in [the classification guide](beer-classification.md) is an explanatory relationship, not a certification. This article is limited to the cited US and BJCP sources; it is not legal advice for exporters or a taxonomy of every national brewing tradition.
