# Measured beer metrics do not define a beverage's identity

## Four numbers answer four different questions

A published beer profile might give gravity, ABV, bitterness units and colour. Those figures can be informative, but none tells the whole story of ingredients, local name, fermentation practice or why a community serves a drink. An open laboratory brewing guide explains gravity as a density-related measure, bitterness as a laboratory or predicted quantity, and ABV as something that may be estimated from gravity changes. Those are separate endpoints, not a single universal definition of beer.[^v116-lab]

A familiar example is colour: the BJCP's colour guide links SRM to the absorbance of blue light at 430 nanometres, while visual colour comparison involves light, viewing conditions and an imperfect printed guide. An SRM range cannot identify malt variety, appearance in every glass or community provenance.[^v116-color]

## Naming authority remains separate

The BJCP beer-evaluation guide is written to help judges describe a particular competition entry in a particular style context. Its tasting structure does not establish that a historically named grain ferment has to meet those numeric judging bounds to retain its own identity.[^v116-bjcpEval] A scientific assay, a retail label, a style-guideline edition and a local practice should be stored as different assertions with different provenance.

When comparing a named tradition with an industrial beer, ask who took the measurement, whether it concerns wort or finished beer, whether a reported value was observed or estimated, and which group supplied the name. See [beer classification](beer-classification.md) and the [gravity evidence guide](../processes/gravity-extract-and-estimated-alcohol.md). This is a way to read evidence without retroactively imposing modern measurements on an undocumented historic drink.
