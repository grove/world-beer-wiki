# Agave, cassava and cereal ferments: distinct knowledge systems

A fermented beverage can be made from sap rich in available sugar, starch requiring conversion, or fruit with additional sweetener. **That biochemical comparison does not make the beverages the same cultural kind.** Mexican pulque begins with aguamiel, whereas Brazilian cauim may involve different cooked agricultural materials, and Nigerian kunun-zaki can be a mildly fermented cereal food-drink.[^v111-pulque-review][^v111-cauim-history][^v111-kunun-study]

## Comparison by process rather than ranking

A useful grid distinguishes substrate; cooking, malting or chewing when documented; inoculation ecology; intended food or drink use; named producer/community; and what evidence was consulted. A laboratory sample should carry its site and date rather than silently supplying a recipe to a different regional article. Pulque review microbiology, sixteenth-century cauim writing and a fortified Nigerian experiment are not interchangeable research designs.[^v111-pulque-review][^v111-cauim-history]

## What remains outside this classification

No article should declare a community's own language for its beverage incorrect because a BJCP beer guideline would classify it differently. Some related ferments are included for comparison, not asserted as beers. Similarly, the lack of a known alcohol value is a research gap rather than proof that a drink has no alcohol. Where community knowledge is sensitive, the right next step may be an attribution and review rather than more extraction of detail.[^v111-kunun-study]
