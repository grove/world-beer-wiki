# A sampled ferment is not a population of brewers

Three kinds of research can all say “traditional beer,” yet answer different questions. A southern Assam study collected **15 beverage samples** across five cohorts; an umqombothi study sequenced several process-state groups; a western Norwegian genomics paper investigated **64 isolates from 25 cultures**. These sample counts are not the number of practices, individuals or recipes in the wider communities.[^v16-assam][^v16-umqom-micro][^v16-kveik-2026]

## Ask what was selected

| Material | Potential inference | What does not follow |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Community-produced drink samples | Composition of selected batches | Every member of the community makes it that way |
| Laboratory isolates | Genomes or measured traits in isolated strains | The original mixed culture behaves exactly like each isolate |
| Amplicon sequences | DNA markers and relative community patterns | Viability, causal function, disease risk or safety |
| First-person field account | A named person's explanation | A universally binding national rule |

## The cultural boundary

Brewing knowledge belongs to people. Research descriptions should identify named communities and actual consent where supported, not treat human practices as automatically inferable from an ingredient or sequence. Findings may be combined only when methods and contexts make that comparison defensible.

## Reading tip

Every substantial claim should carry its own source and an access note. An abstract, a full paper and a producer account are valuable for different questions; none supplies a blank check for unrelated assertions.
