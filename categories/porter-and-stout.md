# Porter and stout

Porter and stout form a useful comparison family, but their names do not provide a timeless division by strength, grain choice or yeast. Start with a dated profile and then ask about roast, sweetness, body and fermentation. The [historical London porter](../styles/historical-london-porter.md) article is the route into documentary history; the modern profiles describe different targets.

The current BJCP English Porter entry explicitly describes a modern version. Its treatment separates that reference from recreations of particular historical periods. Reading a contemporary porter specification back into an eighteenth-century brewing record would lose that distinction.[^bjcp-2021-13c]

## Compare along several dimensions

| Profile | What to compare |
|---|---|
| [English Porter](../styles/english-porter.md) | Moderate roast with chocolate, caramel or bread-like malt; a modern reference, not every historical porter. [^bjcp-2021-13c] |
| [American Porter](../styles/american-porter.md) | More assertive roast and often more hop presence than the English reference. [^bjcp-2021-20a] |
| [Baltic Porter](../styles/baltic-porter.md) | Strong, rich regional interpretations with smooth fermentation; a bridge to the lager discussion. [^bjcp-2021-9c] |
| [Irish Stout](../styles/irish-stout.md) | Coffee-like roast and a dry-to-balanced finish; dispense can change the texture. [^bjcp-2021-15b] |
| [Sweet Stout](../styles/sweet-stout.md) | A sweeter roast balance, often involving lactose. [^bjcp-2021-16a] |
| [Oatmeal Stout](../styles/oatmeal-stout.md) | Oats and roast contribute to a distinct balance; it is not simply a synonym for sweet stout. [^bjcp-2021-16b] |
| [Imperial Stout](../styles/imperial-stout.md) | Greater strength and intensity, with substantial latitude in roast, malt and hops. [^bjcp-2021-20c] |

For further branches, compare [Irish Extra Stout](../styles/irish-extra-stout.md), [Foreign Extra Stout](../styles/foreign-extra-stout.md), and [Tropical Stout](../styles/tropical-stout.md). Their separate articles keep strength, export history and regional interpretation visible instead of treating “foreign” and “tropical” as interchangeable descriptions.

## Texture is not an ingredient list

The tight head and creamy impression of a nitrogen-dispensed Irish stout partly reflect serving method. BJCP warns against expecting the same texture from a normally bottled example. “Creamy” in a tasting note therefore does not by itself identify dairy ingredients.[^bjcp-2021-15b]

The [oats](../ingredients/oats.md), [barley and malt](../ingredients/barley-and-malt.md), and [sugars](../ingredients/sugars.md) references explain ingredient roles. Use actual recipe or producer evidence when claiming a beer contains lactose, unmalted roasted barley, oats or coffee. A sensory resemblance is not an ingredient declaration.

## Modern additions and historical labels

[Pastry stout](../styles/pastry-stout.md) is an evolving dessert-oriented family; its profile distinguishes the label from the presence of any one adjunct. [Wood-aged beer](../styles/wood-aged-beer.md) adds another descriptive dimension, with a separate [specialty wood-aged](../styles/specialty-wood-aged-beer.md) entry where prior barrel contents shape the result.

For an informative comparison, record the base family, period, fermentation, flavor additions and maturation separately. A stronger or sweeter contemporary beer is not automatically more faithful to an older “stout” name. Historical claims need historical evidence; the [history guide](historical-and-revival-beers.md) explains that boundary.
