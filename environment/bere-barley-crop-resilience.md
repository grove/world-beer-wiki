# Bere barley resilience and island landscapes: potential is not a footprint score

## A landrace's traits belong to a place and farming system

Martin and colleagues' open review describes Bere as a long-maintained barley landrace suited to aspects of northern Scottish-island farming. It examines the crop's role in low-input conditions, local cropping systems and conservation alongside changes in markets. The review describes potential resilience traits; it does not calculate a universal greenhouse-gas advantage per litre of beer or prove that Bere outyields every modern barley in every region.[^v117-bere-review]

The University of the Highlands and Islands research overview points to evaluation of Bere genetics and incorporation of selected traits into breeding for demanding environments. This is evidence of an institutional programme and its research aims, not a commercially verified performance score for every hybrid line.[^v117-bere-institute]

## Climate, geography and what can be transferred

Island soil properties, growing season, seed availability, fertiliser and traditional land use interact. A favorable trait in one coastal or manganese-deficient soil setting may require a separate agronomic trial elsewhere. Conserving a crop in a landscape is also different from a life-cycle claim about the environmental burdens of processing, packaging and transport.

For any footprint comparison, state harvest year, field yield, inputs, upstream stages, functional unit and uncertainty. Neither the word heritage nor the word resilient eliminates the need for quantitative evidence.
