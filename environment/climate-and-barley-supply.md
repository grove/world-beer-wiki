# Extreme weather and the barley-to-beer supply chain

Barley is an agricultural crop before it is a brewing ingredient. A Nature Plants modeling paper coupled crop yields with trade and economic models to explore what simultaneous heat and drought extremes could mean for barley availability and beer supply.[^v14-barley-climate]

## A scenario is not a forecast for your pint

The authors estimated global average barley-yield losses between 3% and 17% across the modeled severity scenarios. Their beer supply and price outcomes also depend on economic assumptions, competing grain uses and consumer markets; these figures are not observed losses in the year 2026.[^v14-barley-climate]

## The grain itself changes

A separate 2024 review highlights that climate, barley genotype and growing conditions affect starch properties and how malting and mashing behave. Ingredient availability and brewing performance are connected but not identical questions.[^v14-starch]

## Edition 1.9: newly sourced connections

An archaeogenetic study of Qasr Ibrim barley illustrates crop variability across long time horizons: ancient grains showed a two-row appearance associated with a six-row ancestral mutation at *vrs1*. That is evidence for a particular agricultural-history question, **not an independent climate-change forecast** or proof that any brewing barley can be bred to withstand projected heat and drought. See [the archaeological case](../history/qasr-ibrim-barley-ancient-dna.md) and [barley row types](../ingredients/barley-row-type-and-brewing-assumptions.md). Modern supply-risk estimates need their own dated field, yield and climate observations.[^v19-nubian-barley]

## Edition 1.17: newly sourced connections

Climate-related crop quality must be described by **stage and mechanism**, not just a projected global yield. Hull and colleagues' 2026 review details pre-harvest sprouting risks linked to rainfall, humidity, cultivar and dormancy. It does not predict the harvest day or malting suitability of a particular future farm lot.[^v117-sprouting-review]

A separate 2026 three-cultivar drought study's PubMed abstract reports grain-composition and malt-quality shifts from water stress during growth. Drought at grain filling and rainfall-driven sprouting near harvest are not identical processes, and neither laboratory endpoint alone establishes a price, brewery carbon footprint or population-wide crop failure.[^v117-drought-study]
