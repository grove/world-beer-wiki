# Moving from winter ice to heat pumps does not give one timeless beer carbon footprint

## The past was not automatically low carbon

Imagine a nineteenth-century brewery using winter ice in an insulated cellar. The Swiss National Museum's history shows that the ice might be cut from a pond, packed, carted, stored and transported by rail before being used. Natural ice was not an energy-free object when people, horses, facilities and infrastructure moved it over distances.[^v119-swissice26]

Norwegian Museum of Science and Technology research describes how ice availability helped Norwegian breweries export beer by ship during 1860–1900. That infrastructure broadened trade possibilities but does not supply a quantitative lifecycle inventory that can be compared directly with today's refrigeration electricity.[^v119-norwayice]

## The present has a different system

A 2026 brewery thermal paper models energy recovery, CO₂-based heat pumps and industrial heat-pump configurations for a representative site. It compares designs using contemporary energy and investment assumptions. A future brewery using renewable electricity may have different emissions from one with fossil-intensive power, yet those values require their own verified input data.[^v119-pinch26]

A story about progress is not itself a comparison of identical functional units. The kind of beer, quantity shipped, heat and ice sources, climate, labor and distribution all change across centuries. “Old ice good, new machines bad” and its reverse both exceed the available evidence.
