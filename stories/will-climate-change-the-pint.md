# Can a changing climate change your beer?

A beer begins in fields and orchards of ingredients—often across different climates. Researchers modeled how simultaneous drought and heat extremes could affect barley supply, while another team measured historical shifts in European hops and projected further risks.[^v14-barley-climate][^v14-hops-climate]

## Two kinds of evidence

The barley analysis combines crop and economic scenarios. The hop paper uses actual regional observations as well as models. Neither tells us what any specific future bottle will taste like; variety choices, sourcing, brewing adjustments and energy systems intervene.[^v14-hops-climate]

## Edition 1.19: newly sourced connections

Brewery decarbonization studies add a further distinction to climate narratives: what a plant *could* save in a model versus what a whole beverage's lifecycle actually emits. Padullés and colleagues' 2026 work develops a generalized brewery heat and electrification system, with model-specific technology choices. That is not a forecast that every beer will be produced with identical electrical demand in any given year.[^v119-pinch26]

The GHG Protocol defines Scope 1, Scope 2 and Scope 3 emission boundaries, including purchased electricity and upstream supply chain impacts. Lower energy demand in the brewhouse does not itself account for barley, hop growing, consumer distribution or packaging.[^v119-ghgfaq]

Keep climate effects on crops, industrial heat options, land use and material transport separate in a story about the future pint. Read [heat-pump evidence](../environment/industrial-heat-pump-model-boundaries.md) and [the emissions scope guide](../environment/scope-1-2-3-brewing-emissions.md). No global climate forecast is inferred from one technical process model.
