# The tablet was not the recipe

Imagine a newly catalogued ancient tablet listing beer for travelers. How many flavors can you taste from it? **None directly.** A CDLI Ur III ration record names beer among supplies assigned to three messengers. It is a remarkable glimpse of a state economy: someone accounted for provisions with enough precision to distinguish goods and recipients.[^v19-cdli-ration]

## The tempting but unjustified leap

The catalog notes two named beer forms. It is tempting to translate them into a familiar modern quality ladder, infer a serving temperature, and reconstruct a beer. But Peter Damerow explains why cuneiform accounting records and the *Hymn to Ninkasi* preserve different kinds of evidence. Ancient technical words do not line up automatically with modern malt, wort or fermentation stages.[^v19-damerow]

## The richer story is more modest

This one text tells us about messengers, travel, distribution and an administrative classification. It does not tell us who brewed their provisions, whether they liked the taste or how a vessel was sterilized. The fact that an archive preserved paperwork but not flavor is itself interesting. A historically responsible reconstruction would declare every experimental assumption rather than calling it the Ur III recipe.[^v19-cdli-ration]
