# The saccharometer made an extract number—not a recipe

## A brewer wants to compare value

A merchant asks which batch gave the most extract from its ingredients. It is tempting to imagine that the answer has always been a natural number waiting to be measured. Historian James Sumner argues, in the public abstract of his study on John Richardson, that a brewing extract quantity was historically shaped around commercial aims, and that the saccharometer helped make it persuasive to its users.[^v116-sumner]

The Wellcome Collection records Richardson's printed tables and directions in 1784. That bibliographic fact is anchored in a dated publication, but the catalogue record alone does not tell us the response of every brewer who encountered the treatise.[^v116-wellcome]

## An instrument survives

The Science Museum Group preserves an improved saccharometer from approximately 1825–1835, manufactured by Dring and Fage. Its weights and thermometer belong to an instrument designed for numerical reading. The museum object shows the physicality of measurement; it is not identified as Richardson's personal possession.[^v116-museum]

## The part the number cannot reveal

Even with an extract reading, one would not know how the beer tasted, who brewed it, what labour went into making it or which gathering it accompanied. Numbers can improve comparisons, but a single number should not replace the human and documentary circumstances of production.
