# Why a fermented drink is not a water-safety certificate

The numbers in a 2020 study are striking. In one Shawi community of the Peruvian Amazon, researchers found coliform contamination in **all 26 tested communal water samples**, compared with **2 of 11 masato samples**. It would be tempting to turn that into a headline saying fermented cassava makes water safe. The original authors did **not** establish that conclusion: they called for further microbiological safety research.[^v18-shawi]

## The sample is also a cultural story

Participatory photographs documented cassava preparation, household work, the stored mash and the drink served with water. The comparison is meaningful partly because masato was already a culturally important drink in that community. Any proposed water intervention must be considered with community participation, not imposed because one lab result looks promising.[^v18-shawi]

## What the measurements cannot rule out

Coliform culture results are one kind of microbial indicator. They do not automatically test for every pathogen, toxin, hazardous preparation condition or long-term health outcome. The study's small number of masato samples, sampling dates and locality all matter. Nor can an observation about this community be rewritten as a rule that every Amazonian masato uses chewing or equivalent fermentation conditions.

## Why uncertainty makes a better story

The important question is what the methods and participants actually show, and what should happen next with community guidance. Read [Shawi masato and daily life](../culture/shawi-masato-and-water.md), [the photographed preparation](../processes/shawi-masato-preparation.md), and the [Masato profile](../styles/masato-de-yuca.md). Fermentation is fascinating without dangerous promises about untreated water.
