# A fungus in barley is not necessarily the fermenter of the beer

## Two reports use the word fungus

A reader finds a paper about Fusarium DNA in barley and another about mixed fermentations. It might be tempting to join them into a story in which Fusarium must be the source of the beer's characteristic fermentation. That inference would exceed either study.

The 2024 Jayathissa trial deliberately compared plant infection by different Fusarium graminearum isolates under field conditions and observed malt-related consequences. It addresses crop pathology and fungal traits, not evidence of a community making a desired starter from the same species.[^v117-fungal-strains]

A 2026 controlled Biehl experiment tracked fungal DNA and particular chemical metabolites through inoculated malt and brewed beer. The analyses indicate that a pathogen's material and metabolites can matter downstream. They do not mean that a positive DNA signal proves a live, beneficial fermenter at each later stage.[^v117-fusarium-trial]

## The missing middle of the story

Cereal grains, native microbes, selected brewing cultures and manufactured malt can intersect. To establish an organism's metabolic role in a finished beverage, a study would need appropriate timepoints and functional tests; cultural practice would additionally require locally grounded testimony. Detection, function and named tradition cannot be collapsed.

The same discipline applies to health claims: a toxin transformation is not a safety clearance or a reason to process suspect grain. A laboratory inoculation study is not a practical brewing recipe.
