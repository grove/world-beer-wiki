# Two beers with the same IBU need not taste equally bitter

## Matching numbers invite the wrong conclusion

Imagine a tasting of two beers displaying the same bitterness units. One might expect the same bitterness on the tongue. But the publisher abstract of an ASBC journal experiment describes individual beers with similar IBU readings and very different amounts of iso-alpha acid. The test also found that aging-hop conditions affected what those readings represented. Those are case-specific experimental contrasts, not universal flavour claims about every beer.[^v116-ibuStudy]

A separate open chemistry review explains how multiple hop-derived and oxidized components can affect bitterness and how non-specific spectrophotometric measurements can register different mixtures. A bittering-unit number is therefore a property of an analytical method applied to a sample, not an unconditional sensory intensity guarantee.[^v116-bitterReview]

The ASBC's public Beer 23 description makes the measurement mechanism concrete: a selected solvent extract is assessed optically at 275 nanometres. The presence of this procedure says nothing, by itself, about how a blinded consumer panel would rank two beers.[^v116-bitterMethod]

## A better ending to the comparison

The cataloger should retain both IBUs, how they were obtained and the conditions of each product. A tasting note can then report the perceived differences without altering the laboratory figures to fit expectations. Hop additions, compound profiles, beer matrix and participant context all remain relevant.

Read [the sensory guide](../sensory/bitterness-is-not-one-number.md), [the hop-fraction chemistry](../ingredients/hop-bitter-fractions-and-analytical-signals.md) and [the laboratory interpretation](../processes/bitterness-units-and-hop-chemistry.md). No home extraction experiment or consumer trial is proposed here.
