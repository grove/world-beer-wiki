# Why 0.0 on a beer label needs a jurisdiction

## The same shelf can hold different kinds of claims

Imagine a cataloger reading “0.0,” “non-alcoholic,” “alcohol free” and “session” on bottles from different markets. The easy but incorrect shortcut is to create one global class and assign every word to it. A number, a regulated descriptor, a brand's chosen language and a judging style can answer entirely different questions.

The US record makes this visible over time. An archived 1991 ATF circular addressed non-alcoholic malt beverage marketing to the regulated trade. It proves that an agency discussed this market then; it does not prove when the category was invented or what every label looked like.[^v115-circular]

Current TTB guidance distinguishes a less-than-0.5% “non-alcoholic” label with a specified disclosure from an “alcohol free” claim requiring no alcohol. The definitions belong to US federally regulated malt beverages, not to every place where a drink can be bought.[^v115-alcohol]

The UK government's England-applicable 2023 consultation asked about changing a recommended threshold, but the act of asking is not the same as adopting a rule. Quoting a consultation as a 2026 legal outcome would reverse the source's meaning.[^v115-uk]

## The useful questions

Which jurisdiction? Which exact label? Is the alcohol figure measured or declared? Which guidance edition and date? What production route, if any, is actually documented? These questions improve [menu interpretation](../food/alcohol-descriptors-on-a-menu.md) without implying an unverified health or safety conclusion.
