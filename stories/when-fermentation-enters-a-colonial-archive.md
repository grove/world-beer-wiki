# When fermentation enters a colonial archive

Imagine a drink appearing twice in the archives: once as an object of religious concern, another time as an item that officials wished to tax. Both records concern pulque, but they do not ask the same question. One historical study interprets clerical transcriptions of maguey incantations and first-pulque practice among Nahua communities; another examines production and revenue administration in Chalco during 1778–1821.[^v111-pulque-ritual][^v111-pulque-tax]

## Two witnesses with different interests

A colonial cleric writing about ritual and a tax official organizing revenue had different incentives to record certain actions and overlook others. A historian reading their accounts must keep those institutional perspectives visible. Neither archive lets the wiki reproduce exact Indigenous ritual instructions or assign all producers one shared history. The dating and locations also differ significantly.[^v111-pulque-ritual]

## The useful surprise

The ferment itself does not reveal who owned the agave, collected the levy, carried the vessel, or decided which practice was legitimate. Those are questions of labor, power and interpretation. The source abstracts invite deeper work with original documents and willing local reviewers. The entertaining story is not a claimed ancient recipe; it is how a single drink can expose contrasting forms of historical evidence.[^v111-pulque-tax]
