# A green bottle alone cannot tell you whether beer has gone stale

## One colour, several possible histories

A reader sees a green glass bottle and decides the beer must have a skunk-like lightstruck aroma. Green or clear glass can transmit more of the light relevant to a familiar beer fault than brown bottles, but the bottle itself is not a record of exposure. Huvaere and colleagues' 2005 chemistry paper demonstrates reaction pathways involving excited riboflavin and hop-derived bittering compounds; it does not say that every green bottle has received enough exposure to form the same amount of lightstruck thiol.[^v118-photochem05]

A 2024 beer-storage review discusses oxidative and other aging pathways involving aldehydes, esters and hop components. These can change over time without being identical to the specific light-triggered sulfur chemistry above.[^v118-aging24]

## Study a beer, not a packaging stereotype

In a 2025 open experiment, one unpasteurized beer was subjected to different storage, filtration and yeast treatments. The results show how later aroma and foam can depend on beer-specific circumstances. They do not offer a universal method for estimating the age of a packaged beer by looking at its bottle colour.[^v118-storage25]

A better question asks which beer was in the package, which exposures actually occurred, how sensory observations were made, and which compounds were measured. This is a story about evidence rather than a guarantee of freshness.
