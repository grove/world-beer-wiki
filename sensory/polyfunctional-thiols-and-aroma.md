# Why thiol aromas are not simply hop-variety labels

Some beer compounds called **polyfunctional thiols** have strong aroma impacts at very low concentrations. Labels such as tropical fruit or blackcurrant may be helpful to a taster, but are not diagnostic proof that a particular hop variety, yeast strain, or added fruit is present. A hop-sample study measured considerable variation by variety, origin and harvest; a controlled fermentation study found that yeast, time and dry hopping changed the molecules produced.[^v18-hop-thiol-survey][^v18-thiol-time]

## Free versus bound odorant

Chemically bound precursors can be present even when the corresponding free compound is scarce. A 2022 study investigated disulfide-bound precursors in malt and hops and their release in specific experiments. Therefore “the hop has a thiol precursor” is different from “the finished beer smells of that compound.”[^v18-thiol-bound]

## The brewer cannot infer the finished result from one input

Aroma extraction, microbial transformation, loss during processing and sensory matrix effects intervene between raw material and glass. Likewise, a descriptive tasting word is not equivalent to an analytical concentration measured by LC-MS/MS. Any exact threshold or causal attribution requires its own cited method and beer matrix.

## Follow the chemistry

Compare [ingredient precursors](../ingredients/polyfunctional-thiol-precursors.md), [yeast timing](../microbiology/thiol-release-and-yeast-timing.md), and [terpenoid transformations](hop-terpenoid-biotransformation.md). Thiols and terpenes are distinct chemical groups even when a drinker describes both as fruitlike.
