# Smoke, roast, baked mash and wood are different clues

A dark beer can look like another dark beer while arriving there through different materials and operations. Start with the question **where in production did the relevant change occur?**, rather than treating color as a complete process description.

## Four routes to investigate

| Clue | Production question | Canonical route |
|---|---|---|
| Smoke | Was grain exposed to smoke during malt preparation? | [Smoked malt](../ingredients/smoked-malt.md) |
| Roast | Which dark or roasted grain materials were used? | [Schwarzbier](../styles/schwarzbier.md) |
| Baked or deeply toasted character | Was the mash itself subjected to additional oven heating? | [Baked mash](../processes/baked-mash.md) |
| Wood-associated character | Did beer contact wood, and what was that wood's previous use? | [Wood-aged beer](../styles/wood-aged-beer.md) |

The existing specialty guide separates smoke-derived ingredient character from wood contact and previous barrel contents. A wood-aged beer need not have been brewed with smoked malt, and a smoked beer need not have matured in a spirits barrel.[^atlas-smoke-context]

The keptinis account adds another route: baking a pale-malt mash produces a substantially darker preparation before wort extraction. Its sensory description belongs to the observed beer, not to all dark beer everywhere.[^atlas-keptinis-context]

## Appearance is a clue, not a verdict

The Schwarzbier reference is a useful counterexample to assumptions that a nearly black beer must be unusually strong, sweet or heavy. Its style description combines dark appearance with a relatively restrained, clean profile.[^atlas-schwarzbier-context]

For a comparison, first record appearance and sensory observations. Then attach actual ingredient and process evidence. An explanation can remain provisional when the producer's method is unknown; there is no need to fill the gap with a plausible-sounding barrel or malt story.

## Evidence note

This guide does not claim each aroma has only one cause or that tasting can uniquely identify a treatment. It organizes existing descriptions into better questions. Exact chemical mechanisms, thresholds and causal sensory comparisons need directly relevant experimental evidence.
