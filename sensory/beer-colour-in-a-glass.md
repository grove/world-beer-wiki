# What the eye sees in a glass is not exactly SRM

## Light path and viewing conditions matter

SRM is a defined optical measurement, not a photograph of a universal beer colour. The BJCP's public guide relates SRM to a 430-nanometre absorbance measurement and uses printed comparisons as approximate aids. A reader judging a deep glass in warm room light is looking at a different presentation than a controlled cuvette in a laboratory.[^v116-color]

An open laboratory protocol uses defined sample preparation and spectrophotometry to assign beer colour. It does not mean a sample's transparency, haze, foam colour or full hue is automatically captured by the same single value.[^v116-lab]

## Appearance can influence expectation

A beer's apparent colour may be part of how a drinker anticipates its flavour. A published consumer-study abstract by Blackmore and colleagues reports context-dependent effects of colour and sensory labels on expectations about regular and non-alcoholic beers. That does not prove every individual makes the same association, or that colour determines the drink's actual ingredients.[^v116-expect]

## More careful descriptions

Instead of inferring a style or malt bill from a picture, note glass geometry, lighting, clarity, foam, sample temperature and whether SRM is an instrument result or a visual guess. An orange, amber or nearly black appearance is a useful sensory observation, but it does not determine fermentation method or brewery geography.

Read [the analytical colour method](../processes/beer-colour-measurement.md), [malt-to-beer context](../ingredients/malt-colour-versus-finished-beer-colour.md) and [expectation effects](expectations-and-beer-colour.md). Visual descriptions here are not grading decisions or claims about product quality.
