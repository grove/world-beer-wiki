# Hot-steep malt sensory tests are not finished-beer preference tests

## A malt sample is not the finished pint

A 2025 BrewingScience article by Häge and colleagues describes combining a hot-steep malt sensory method with a Check-All-That-Apply exercise. Their selected samples included three heritage spring barleys and one modern comparison, all malted in a standardized small-scale setting. The abstract presents this as a way to distinguish certain malt character attributes with trained panelists, not as a blind mass-market test of which final beer is better.[^v117-hot-steep]

A separate open review of Scottish Bere describes a regionally conserved barley landrace used in food and drinks. The Bere paper concerns local cultivation, diverse uses and agronomic traits; it does not identify the German sensory study's named heritage varieties as Bere, nor does it provide results from a finished Bere beer tasting.[^v117-bere-review]

## Keep sample and inference boundaries visible

A sensory attribute detected in a malt extract can help guide analytical questions about the raw material. Brewing later adds mashing, boiling, fermentation, carbonation and service conditions. The final beverage therefore should not inherit all malt-panel results as unconditional tasting notes.

The study's published abstract does not expose every individual assessor result or reproducibility protocol. Panel training, grain harvest year and sample processing are important for interpreting even a promising discrimination method. No community's traditional evaluation framework is replaced by this laboratory task.
