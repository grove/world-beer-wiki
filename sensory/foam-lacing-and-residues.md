# Foam lacing, glass residue and competing explanations

## What lacing can tell a server

Residual rings of foam and the adherence of bubbles to glass walls are part of how people evaluate a beer's presentation. The Colorado Brewers Guild describes observational checks for beer-clean glassware, including lacing and water behavior. Its examples are practical service cues; they are not definitive chemical identification or microbiological tests.[^v115-glass]

## An independent physical mechanism

Liger-Belair and Cilindre studied how CO2 emerges from tiny gas pockets, particles and microcrevices. Their results make it clear that nucleation is a physical process with several dependencies. Because some glass features can deliberately support streams of bubbles, bubble placement alone does not reliably discriminate deliberate nucleation from residue.[^v115-bubbles]

Two distinct explanations can coexist: a dirty surface can interfere with foam behavior, while a purpose-designed glass feature can promote bubbling in a clean vessel. Ingredient-derived proteins and hop compounds also affect foam behavior; a glass observation cannot isolate their contributions without a controlled comparison.

## Better description, fewer premature diagnoses

Record **lacing pattern, bubble location, glass type, service conditions and plausible competing explanations**. Do not turn a photograph into a claim about a specific detergent, sanitizer, bacterial organism or brewery fault. A repeated observation in controlled glassware would be stronger evidence than one consumer impression.
