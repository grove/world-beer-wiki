# Nitrogenated beer, foam and visual perception

Creamy-looking nitrogenated draught beer is partly a story about bubbles and flow. A 2019 experiment found that the downward-looking cascade in Guinness can be explained by gravity-current instability and circulation in the container.[^v14-bubbles]

## Why the glass shape matters

The researchers used sloped and rectangular test vessels to observe changing bubble distributions, separating apparent motion from the upward buoyancy of individual bubbles. The geometry matters; the result is not a law that every dark beer must settle in this way.[^v14-bubbles]

## Perception is another question

Visual texture and foam can shape expectation, but this experiment did not test enjoyment or a particular flavor molecule. Read [nitrogenated dispense](../objects/nitrogenated-draught-and-bubbles.md) and [Czech foam pours](../objects/czech-foam-pours.md) as two distinct service traditions.

## Edition 1.15: newly sourced connections

The familiar visual behavior of beer bubbles is not one universal carbonation test. A 2021 ACS Omega study investigated CO2 bubble nucleation after pouring a commercial lager, including dissolved gas, microcrevices and modeled site geometry. Its assumptions differ from nitrogenated dispense and deliberately nucleated glassware; it cannot be applied as a total-bubble benchmark for every foam system.[^v115-bubbles]

A glass-service guide from the Colorado Brewers Guild uses lacing and adhering bubbles as cues for possible surface residue, while physical models show additional mechanisms for nucleation. Appearance alone therefore does not establish a cleaning fault, an ingredient fault or a standard gas blend. The proper question is which **gas, beverage, vessel, conditions and evidence method** were observed.[^v115-glass]
