# A falling gravity reading does not identify a microorganism

## A chemical change is not a species name

Gravity readings during fermentation track bulk density, which can change as fermentable components are consumed and ethanol and carbon dioxide are produced. Thesseling and colleagues' laboratory brewing paper describes sample measurement and fermentation monitoring, but a density curve is not a genetic or microbiological identification of the organism causing the change.[^v116-lab]

A finished beer can show a similar original-to-final gravity difference under different combinations of yeast strain, wort composition, process history and residual solids. A particular attenuation value therefore cannot prove a wild-yeast species, mixed starter recipe, bacterial composition or cultural history.

## Multiple questions need multiple methods

The ASBC/BA public method series distinguishes density and apparent extract measurement from acidity and pH testing. That is already enough to show that no single instrument reads the full process state. A microbial identity claim would require organism-specific evidence rather than an inferred story from these bulk chemistry measurements.[^v116-methodVideos]

A research account should specify the sampled batch, time relative to fermentation, whether the observation is laboratory-controlled or community-reported, and whether a claim concerns isolated organisms, DNA signals or demonstrated activity. Don't convert a time-series trend into a universal recipe or food safety judgment.

See [fermentation timepoints](fermentation-timepoints-and-inference.md), [gravity and attenuation](../processes/gravity-extract-and-estimated-alcohol.md) and [microbial succession](microbial-succession.md). This guide is about inference boundaries and contains no culture-growth or pathogen-handling instructions.
