# Saccharomyces cerevisiae in brewing

*Saccharomyces cerevisiae* is a yeast species, not a single flavor profile. The existing [brewing yeast overview](../ingredients/brewing-yeast.md) explains why strain, nutrient environment and fermentation practice must accompany the species name when describing an actual beer.[^atlas-yeast-overview]

## A concrete example: kveik

Preiss and colleagues investigated nine Norwegian source cultures and selected isolates. Their 2018 work placed the studied kveik yeasts within domesticated *S. cerevisiae* and identified a genetically distinct group with noteworthy brewing traits. Many tested isolates combined rapid fermentation, thermotolerance or flocculation characteristics, but the study did not establish one identical phenotype for every farmhouse culture.[^atlas-kveik-2018]

The result is useful precisely because it links particular material to measurements. “Contains *S. cerevisiae*” would be a much less informative description of those cultures than the study's strain-level findings.

## Four records worth keeping

| Record | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Species identification | Names the organism at a broad taxonomic level. |
| Strain or isolate identifier | Connects measurements to the tested material. |
| Source culture and provenance | Preserves what the brewer actually maintained. |
| Conditions and observed behavior | Prevents a result from becoming an unconditional promise. |

A commercial isolate can be useful without reproducing every organism or working practice in its source culture. Read [Kveik](../ingredients/kveik.md) for the cultural and brewing context rather than treating the species name as a substitute for it.

## Evidence note

No optimal temperature, universal attenuation or mandatory aroma is assigned to the entire species here. Compare [lager hybrids](lager-yeast-hybrids.md) before assuming ale/lager labels are a complete biological taxonomy. The article is explanatory, not an isolation or culture-maintenance protocol.
