# A nineteenth-century saccharometer as a material object

## What survives in the collection

The Science Museum Group catalogues an improved saccharometer made by Dring and Fage in Bermondsey around 1825–1835. Its object record identifies a brass instrument, a case, weights, a slide rule and a thermometer; the museum assigns it object number 1954-382. This is evidence for a particular manufactured apparatus and its catalogued date range, not proof that every British brewhouse owned or used the same design.[^v116-museum]

The museum image carries licensing conditions distinct from the factual object metadata. This wiki links to the collection entry instead of copying an image or claiming rights to reproduce it.

## Measurement and authority

Historian James Sumner's published abstract examines how John Richardson's saccharometry helped make an extract measure persuasive to commercial brewers in the eighteenth century. A surviving later instrument belongs to that longer material history without establishing a direct manufacturer-to-inventor chain.[^v116-sumner]

A saccharometer could make an otherwise invisible quantity legible as a scale reading, but an instrument is not a transcript of a recipe or a census of actual practice. Its commercial use would depend on what brewers chose to measure, the units available and the interpretation of the reading.

To compare the object with later scientific reporting, read [modern hydrometer conditions](hydrometers-and-reading-conditions.md) and [Richardson's historical research](../history/richardson-saccharometry-and-extract.md). The museum's dates are approximate, so this is not given a fabricated annual celebration or a precise single day in the atlas calendar.
