# The white flag outside a Piura chichería

In a 2008 ethnoarchaeological report from northern Peru, a white flag fixed to a long pole outside a household brewery indicated that chicha was being served. The signal makes commercial brewing visible in the streetscape even though the actual production takes place behind a home’s walls. It links an everyday object with time, reputation and local knowledge.[^v17-piura]

## Service signs are evidence, not style markers

The study’s interviews were with women from ten chicherías around Chulucanas. Some sellers maintained known weekly schedules. A passing customer could use a sign and habitual timing to find a maker; neither the sign nor the days tell us the precise fermentation culture or grain blend in the vessel.[^v17-piura]

## Archaeological caution

A perishable flag is not likely to survive archaeologically in the same way as a hearth or ceramic vessel. Its modern use should therefore help researchers think about **missing evidence**: how could ancient roadside advertising have worked, and how much social organization is invisible to excavation? The study explicitly cautions against projecting present-day local patterns backwards as a historical fact.[^v17-piura]

## A useful comparison

Compare this signal with Zoigl’s star in Germany. Both mark service, but the economic organization, regional terminology and evidence are different. The resemblance is a question about how households communicate availability, not proof of transmission between the Andes and Bavaria.
