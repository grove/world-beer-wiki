# Jiahu pottery: when the vessel becomes a laboratory sample

## Ceramic surfaces can retain old chemistry

The 2004 Jiahu research extracted organic material absorbed into pottery vessels and considered chemical signatures alongside archaeobotanical findings. In such contexts the object is both an archaeological artifact and a sample substrate. A chemical match does not label every ingredient unambiguously, and a shattered vessel cannot reveal the exact timing of a lost fermentation.[^v113-jiahu-2004]

## Microscopic remains offer other tests

The 2026 reassessment examined preserved starch granules, phytoliths and fungal microfossils associated with pottery residues. Its interpretation of processing damage and Monascus-related structures changes the questions researchers can ask about starch breakdown. Nevertheless, contamination, preservation, comparative reference collections and the selected vessels remain relevant limits on interpreting a microscopic observation as a historical practice.[^v113-jiahu-2026]

## One jar cannot stand in for a civilization

Archaeological evidence has a location, period and sample-selection history. Linking Jiahu pottery with later bronze vessels, Shangshan or Mijiaya is helpful for comparing methods and chronology, but does not make their contents the same style. The objects tell us more than an entertaining origin story precisely because researchers can ask what is directly measured and what remains a reasoned inference.[^v113-jiahu-2004][^v113-jiahu-2026]
