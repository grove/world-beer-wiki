# Microscopic nucleation sites in a beer glass

## Why a surface matters

A beer glass is not a chemically irrelevant backdrop. Liger-Belair and Cilindre investigated how gas pockets retained in microcrevices can promote CO2 bubble nucleation after a lager is poured. In their model, a sufficiently sized site and sufficient dissolved gas are prerequisites for bubbling; sites with different dimensions behave differently.[^v115-bubbles]

The paper tested a specified lager and controlled glass conditions and included simplifying assumptions in its bubble-number model. Real vessels have a spread of imperfections, particles, temperature histories and pouring conditions. It would be unsound to translate one model's estimated bubble count into an objective rating for a commercial glass.

## What the observer can and cannot see

The Colorado Brewers Guild's service guide uses surface-related observations to prompt cleaning checks. That practical guidance and the ACS paper answer different questions: one concerns routine presentation, the other a modeled physical mechanism. They cannot be reduced to “bubbles mean dirty,” especially where a vessel has deliberately designed nucleation sites.[^v115-glass]

Catalogs can record glass type, deliberately added nucleation features when documented, service procedure and **the evidence for an assertion**. A visual judgment alone cannot identify the exact chemistry of residue, prove microbial contamination, or demonstrate a beer's style.
