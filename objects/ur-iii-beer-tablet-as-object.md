# Beer in clay: a tablet as a working document

A Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative catalog entry identifies an Ur III clay tablet listing provisions for three messengers, including two kinds of beer. For a modern reader, the artifact is a reminder that **beer can survive in the archaeological record as bookkeeping** even when no physical sample of the ancient liquid survives. The medium preserves transactions and categories, not necessarily their sensory meaning.[^v19-cdli-ration]

## What is materially present?

The catalog identifies an artifact and supplies interpretation and links; this page does not claim to have inspected original wedges, photogrammetry or the full transliteration. An inscription can testify to the use of a term by an administration, the amount recorded and the people named, while other aspects of life remain invisible. The record should be read alongside independent textual scholarship where available.[^v19-cdli-ration]

## Archive design shapes what survives

Peter Damerow distinguishes administrative, literary and archaeological evidence. A ration list is unusually strong evidence of distribution, weaker evidence of brewing operations, and almost no direct evidence of finished-beer taste. That hierarchy is crucial when converting a historical document into a structured wiki fact: specify *issued to* or *accounted as*, not “made with this recipe.”[^v19-damerow]
