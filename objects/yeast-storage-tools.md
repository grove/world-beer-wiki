# Yeast rings, logs and storage containers

A culture can survive between brewing sessions through a material practice as well as through a biological lineage. In the Gjernes account, Garshol describes a wooden yeast ring and a refrigerated jar of maintained kveik. The account also identifies a family connection through which replacement culture could be obtained.[^atlas-voss-field]

At Holo, Per Dale recalled his grandfather's use of a **kveikstokk**, or yeast log, a straw ring and sometimes flatbread for drying culture. This is reported family memory, not the same evidential situation as observing those historical objects in active use.[^atlas-holo]

## The object and the organism need separate histories

An old ring does not establish that its present microbes are equally old. A culture's continued use does not prove that every strain remained unchanged. A photographed tool also cannot supply a microbial identification that the source never performed.

For a good object record, preserve the maker or custodian where publicly documented, material and function, whether use was observed or remembered, and how the account relates the object to culture maintenance. Avoid assigning an archaeological age from appearance alone.

## Follow the knowledge, not only the hardware

[Kveik](../ingredients/kveik.md) supplies the culture context; [Vossaøl](../styles/vossaol.md) supplies a named brewing practice. [Mising starter knowledge](../culture/mising-starter-knowledge.md) offers a different example of maintaining and exchanging fermentation material. The comparison concerns transmission, not interchangeable tools or common ancestry.

## Evidence note

The field accounts' storage anecdotes are not validated shelf-life specifications. This page does not recommend tasting an unknown culture to establish safety or using an old object without appropriate assessment. Nor does it claim that a single commercial isolate preserves every organism and practice associated with a family culture.
