# Research and evidence method

## What this edition is

This is a researched reference corpus, not a database of recipes and not an independently reviewed historical encyclopedia. The writer consulted official classification pages, original research, firsthand producer accounts, community documentation, and original historical analysis. The article text is newly written synthesis rather than copied guideline prose.

The research date records when sources were consulted. It does not mean that every beverage was independently observed in production on that date, or that every underlying publication was newly issued then.

## Keep different kinds of knowledge separate

A **style description** summarizes the intended character of a named category. A **tradition profile** discusses local brewing knowledge and variation. A **specialty category** groups beers by ingredients, fermentation, or treatment. A **historical profile** describes dated evidence. An **archaeological profile** interprets material traces, not a surviving menu or recipe. **Related ferments** are included for comparison without asserting that their own communities call them beer.

These distinctions appear in `type` and `scope`. Do not collapse them into one undifferentiated list of universally recognized beer styles.

## How sources are used

**Official style guidelines** supply a consistent baseline for contemporary sensory descriptions and category boundaries. They are primary evidence of what a judging organization defines, not independent proof of every origin story mentioned in a guideline. Most conventional-style entries use BJCP's 2021 edition; that edition is identified explicitly rather than silently updated or blended with another system.

**Original research papers** can document ingredients, processes, samples, and microbial findings. A study of a few makers is not a census of an entire region. The articles avoid promoting sample-specific alcohol, acidity, or microbial measurements into mandatory style limits.

**Community and field accounts** can document how people make, name, and understand a drink. Their local focus is a strength, but it is still a scope boundary. Producer accounts are similarly useful for their own brewing practice; an originator's claim is attributed rather than automatically treated as an independently settled invention history.

**Historical analysis** is used where the author examines early writing or brewing records. The original records were not all independently inspected for this edition. When the profile follows a historian's transcription or interpretation, the source is named as such.

Some source access was limited to an abstract or indexed excerpt. Those limits are recorded in `sources[].access_scope`, `evidence_basis`, or the evidence note. These articles intentionally make fewer claims.

## Fact-checking choices

Definitions and process descriptions were checked against the cited source material. Unsupported precision was removed: there is no invented first-brewing date, universal alcohol range, exact historical flavor, or direct ancestry added merely to make a page feel complete. Where the evidence did not provide a tasting profile, the file says so.

The corpus does not equate modern simulations with ancient recipes, commercial yeast names with complete regional styles, or a common ingredient with cultural identity. A comparison link means “read together,” not “descended from.”

## Reading citations

A footnote after a paragraph identifies the source supporting that paragraph's factual description. Its identifier matches a `sources` entry in the same file. The source notes provide the title, author, link, consultation date, and access scope. Short editorial cautions may draw a logical boundary around what that source can support rather than quote the author's own wording.

## Review status and corrections

All profiles are research drafts. Source checking by the authoring AI is not represented as an independent historian's, brewer's, or community representative's approval. Structural validation checks files and links; it cannot prove historical truth.

A correction should identify the profile, the disputed sentence, the source and precise locator, and the scope of the replacement claim. Preserve conflicting evidence where it matters. Prefer narrowing a claim over silently generalizing one local observation.
