# About this edition {#edition .unnumbered}

*The Beer Detective* is a free, original narrative nonfiction book developed from the research routes in [World Beer Wiki](https://github.com/grove/world-beer-wiki). Its research baseline is edition 1.19, repository commit `3b985c19a9dcccef8d334be54b5405e42093e812`.

Original prose and original vector cover compositions are submitted under the repository's existing Apache License, Version 2.0. The complete license accompanies this edition. No separate Creative Commons license is asserted. Quotations and cited publications retain their respective rights; no third-party photographs or source articles are reproduced. EB Garamond and Bricolage Grotesque font software is used under the SIL Open Font License, with notices supplied in the project package.

Research, drafting, internal editing and production were assisted by AI. This review edition awaits independent human editorial, specialist and community review. It draws on published work rather than new interviews or field visits. The research note and chapter notes describe its method, source access and remaining limits.

No ISBN has been assigned. This book is an investigation of history, science and culture, rather than a brewing or water-treatment manual.

# The Case for Beer {#introduction .unnumbered}

A glass can smell of bananas even when no banana went near the brewery. The resemblance is convincing. The conclusion is wrong.

That small discrepancy is a good reason to begin an investigation. A drink arrives with information already attached to it: a name, a color, an ingredient list, perhaps a story about a monk or a king. Then it supplies information of its own. Something rises from the glass and suggests a fruit. Something on the tongue seems to confirm a number printed on the label. The evidence appears to agree, until someone changes the light, hides the label or asks the yeast what it has been making.[^intro-1]

Beer rewards this sort of interference. Its apparent simplicity gives us plenty to get wrong. Grain, water and fermentation can be described in a sentence; accounting for what happens between the field and the glass takes rather longer. There are organisms doing chemical work, people deciding which work is desirable, and institutions deciding who may do it. The result is a remarkably convenient meeting place for different kinds of knowledge. A chemist and a historian can disagree about entirely different things while studying the same bottle.

They also inherit different kinds of evidence. The chemist may be able to make another batch. The historian cannot ask an eighteenth-century brewer to repeat the day on which a famous beer was supposedly invented. An archaeologist may have the container without the contents. A researcher studying a household business may find that a published price records the finished drink while leaving out hours spent carrying its ingredients. The problem is often to recognize which part of an event has survived as a fact.

This book follows twenty-four such problems. They range from a hollow in a prehistoric stone to a modern experiment in expectation. The route passes through poems, law courts, cold rooms and kitchens. Along the way, a number on a label becomes less informative, a dry account becomes more interesting, and a famous date acquires a longer history on either side of it. Some cases have clear chemical resolutions. Others end with an interpretation that remains open to argument. Both can make satisfying reading, provided the question is worth asking.

There is a temptation, when collecting these stories, to choose the most spectacular claim in each source and hurry on. The oldest beer. The first brewer. The ingredient that changed everything. Such claims supply their own headlines and spare the reader an untidy middle. They also have a way of becoming less interesting under examination. The question of whether beer caused agriculture is narrower than the question of what people knew how to do with wild plants. A single inventor can obscure the more absorbing business of how a new product became possible, profitable and recognizable.

The people come into better focus when the headline loosens its grip. An official's record of an offense can preserve a working woman's refusal. A communal brewhouse can turn out to be only one address in a drink's production. A starter analyzed in a laboratory can contain more kinds of life than a familiar strain name suggests. These discoveries redirect the inquiry toward a sign taken down, liquid carried to another building, and organisms whose work overlaps.[^intro-2]

Nor does every fermented drink in these pages need to be squeezed into the same category. Names carry local distinctions. Pito, chicha, makgeolli, mbege and masato enter the book through particular studies and accounts, with different ingredients, histories and social settings. Similar questions can be asked of them without proposing a shared origin or treating one as an imperfect version of another. A useful comparison keeps the differences visible.

The itinerary moves through places where ancient evidence and present-day choices coexist. A household enterprise is a current arrangement of work and money, with decisions still to be made. Its maker may have reasons to change a method, keep it, or use different methods for different purposes. The laboratory can help explain a transformation; the people doing the work can explain what they want it to achieve. A living tradition has a future under discussion as well as a past.

The reader will encounter some technical terms. They earn their place by solving a problem. An enzyme becomes useful when we need to understand how grain can yield fermentable sugar. A hybrid matters when a yeast's family history refuses to fit a single branch. A statistical comparison matters when a result that failed to appear is as revealing as one that did. There is no examination at the end, and very little reward for memorizing the vocabulary before meeting the evidence.

The book's four parts change the vantage point. First come things that survived after the drink was gone. Then we look inside the glass, following processes too small or too rapid to see unaided. The third part follows the people and arrangements that make a drink available. The last returns to stories that remain powerful even after their foundations have been questioned, before looking toward the energy and crops on which future brewing will depend.

World Beer Wiki supplied the initial map. The route then followed papers, translations, museum records and local accounts, all identified in the notes. Readers who want to examine a disputed interpretation or follow a promising detour can begin there. The research note explains how those sources were used.

The detective in the title has no superior sense of smell and no secret archive. The useful equipment is ordinary: attention to dates, a willingness to compare explanations, and enough patience to notice when a source has changed the subject. It helps to be curious about a failed prediction. It helps even more to enjoy a story after discovering that its most familiar version was mistaken.

The banana will return. First, there is a stone.
