# 16. A Flag Outside the House

The flag was white in the northern Peruvian account. In the southern account, the marker was red. Both announced that chicha was for sale.

It is a small difference, but it stops an easy mistake. There is no need to decide which researcher found the authentic color. Frances Hayashida was investigating production around Chulucanas, in Piura. Felipe Vargas Faulbaum was writing about communities in the Cusco region, including Huarocondo. The places were different. The signs belonged to local ways of making a household business visible.[^c16-1]

A stranger who knew only that chicha was a maize drink would have missed much of what the flag communicated. It announced availability. It pointed to a particular maker and a place where people could enter. It belonged to a timetable understood by customers, and to a relationship between a household and the street. It was a sign for a drink, but also an instruction about how to find it.

Hayashida's 2008 study interviewed women from ten chicherías, or household breweries, in Chulucanas and neighboring settlements. Producers had known sales days. The published methods give La Loba's Tuesdays and Wednesdays and La Cartola's Saturdays and Sundays as examples. Those modest calendar entries make the business concrete: the drink did not appear everywhere at once, as though a village had one communal tap.[^c16-2]

A regular customer could combine the day, the location and the visible signal. An archaeological excavation many centuries later would have a harder time. A pole might leave little behind; a flag might leave nothing recognizable. The timetable would be absent altogether. What had organized access to the drink could disappear while the vessels used to prepare it survived.

This was part of the reason for Hayashida's investigation. Archaeologists could recognize impressive drinking vessels and discuss feasts, yet production itself was harder to identify. Observing living brewers offered a way to connect tasks with the material traces they might leave. The exercise was not to find people unchanged since antiquity. It was to learn what questions to ask of a site whose workers could no longer explain it.

That difference matters. An analogy proposes that a feature might be explained by a certain activity. It becomes useful when the proposal can be checked against other evidence. It becomes dangerous when a present-day observation is silently converted into a photograph of the past. The same-shaped vessel might serve a different purpose; the same purpose might be achieved with different equipment. A good comparison creates possibilities and tests, rather than delivering instant ancestors.

The north-coast study supplies an especially useful clue about work done elsewhere. In the Chulucanas area, makers could purchase ground malted maize, called *pachucho*, and ordinary maize flour from vendors or mills. Brewing in a household therefore did not imply that every preparation happened inside it. A search confined to one house could miss part of the production chain.

This changes how to read an absent tool. If no milling equipment appears in a place used for brewing, the missing equipment need not prove that no grain was processed for the drink. The processing might have occurred elsewhere. Conversely, finding a mill does not prove that all its output became beer. The task is to reconstruct movements among places, not assemble a compulsory collection of objects beside every hearth.

The finished drink conceals these movements rather efficiently. A customer sees a serving vessel and a transaction. The grain may have passed through more hands and more decisions than the room reveals. The phrase *household brewery* identifies a center of activity; it does not mean a sealed little economy.

In Vargas Faulbaum's Cusco-region account, the work becomes personal through a named interlocutor, Engracia Surco Escobedo. His narrative follows activities around maize and chicha, including the use of an electric mill. In Huarocondo he describes red markers outside selling houses. The ethnographer's observations connect the serving room to agricultural work and to changes in the tools people use.[^c16-3]

The electric mill is an important detail because it refuses to cooperate with a picturesque idea of tradition. A visitor might hope for an ancient stone implement. A producer may have good reason to prefer a machine that does the necessary job. The replacement changes the material evidence, but does not settle by itself whether the knowledge, product or social setting has been abandoned.

That is a question to investigate with makers, rather than resolve by ranking tools according to their usefulness in a photograph. A new vessel may alter heat transfer or durability; a new mill may alter the organization of work. The meaningful inquiry is what changed for the people doing the task. *Modern* and *traditional* are too blunt to answer it without further description.

Names also require care. *Chicha* can lead a reader toward a family resemblance, but cannot provide a complete recipe for everything called by that name. *Chicha de jora* directs attention toward germinated maize. Germination helps make starch available for conversion into sugars, yet the grain's later treatment still matters. Knowing that a drink begins with maize is no more sufficient than knowing that bread begins with flour.[^c16-4]

The distinction between chichería and picantería brings the investigation into another set of documents. Peru's Ministry of Culture recognized the cultural spaces of Piura's picanterías and chicherías in a resolution dated 4 November 2015. Its account distinguishes establishments centered on preparing and selling food with chicha from those centered on making and selling chicha. A picantería need not produce the drink it serves. The official text also records the white flag as an announcement of availability.[^c16-5]

The legal document corroborates a practical detail in the field study, while looking at the subject for a different purpose. Hayashida wanted to understand production and its traces. The ministry wanted to identify and recognize cultural practices. Neither purpose is identical to a brewer's need to have a batch ready for customers. Read together, the accounts show several institutions looking at the same drink from different positions.

The recognition was deliberately regional. The resolution describes the decision to issue differentiated declarations because the places, utensils and practices were not identical across the country. That is a useful refusal of tidiness. A national appreciation need not require a national recipe. Even an official category can acknowledge that its meaning becomes clearer when the map is made more precise.

There is another detail in the ministry's announcement that broad histories can easily lose: the women representing these establishments had names. Its report of the recognition ceremony identifies, among others, Susana Victoria Yovera Siesquen and Ingly Quiroga Flores, representing Lambayeque and Piura respectively. They appear as participants in the public recognition of working institutions, rather than as anonymous figures decorating a general story about heritage.[^c16-6]

An award cannot tell us everything about a working day. Recognition does not specify income, guarantee the continuation of a business or settle who has authority over change. But a public document can still preserve which practices an institution chose to value and which people stood for them on that occasion. Those are historical facts worth keeping at their proper scale.

Across these accounts, women's work is repeatedly visible. That should neither disappear into an impersonal phrase such as *the production of chicha* nor expand into a claim that every task, everywhere, belongs to women. The proper unit is the documented setting: these makers, these households, this division of work where it is described. Generalization is least helpful when it takes the place of meeting the people who could complicate it.

A particularly revealing question is what a future observer would get wrong if only the serving vessels survived. Perhaps they would imagine a self-contained brewery where materials had actually been purchased. Perhaps they would assign one permanent function to a room that changed use during the week. Perhaps they would recognize consumption easily and underestimate all the labor before it. These are hypotheses prompted by the living evidence, not deductions about any specific ancient site.

We can also reverse the question. What would an account miss if it preserved only the recipe? It would lose the sales days, the route to the mill, the signals outside a door and the distinction between making a drink and serving someone else's. Much of the organization would vanish, even though every ingredient remained faithfully listed.

The white flag and the red marker help recover that organization because they belong to an exchange. Someone has prepared something and is ready to offer it; someone else knows how to read the invitation. Their significance depends on a place and its people. Detached from those relationships, the flag would be an oddly uninformative souvenir.[^c16-7]

No single color opens every door. The point of learning the sign is to find the right one.
