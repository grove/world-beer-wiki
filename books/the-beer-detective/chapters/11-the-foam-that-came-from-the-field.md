# 11. The Foam That Came from the Field

The barley experiment seemed to offer a straightforward route from cause to effect. Grow two cultivars. Expose them to different strains of a fungal pathogen. Harvest the grain, malt it, and measure what happens. If the fungus is associated with beer that gushes out of its container, perhaps more fungus will mean more gushing.

The results were less obliging. In field trials conducted from 2019 to 2021, the relationships among disease, fungal abundance, toxin measurements and gushing did not move as one. Some outcomes depended on cultivar or pathogen strain. Gushing varied among years, but the researchers did not find a significant relationship with the abundance of the particular fungus they were tracking. Their deliberately inoculated barley did not produce malt with greater gushing activity than the uninoculated comparison.[^c11-1]

It is a good place to enter the foam mystery because it prevents an easy answer from hardening too soon. A bottle that erupts is spectacularly informative about one thing: gas has escaped into bubbles rapidly enough to carry liquid with it. It is far less informative about the chain of events that made this happen. A wet table cannot identify a fungal species.

The gas is usually not a stranger to the beer. Carbon dioxide can remain dissolved under the conditions inside a sealed package. Opening changes the pressure conditions, and the liquid now contains more dissolved gas than it would retain at equilibrium with the surrounding air. Some of that gas escapes at the liquid's surface; some can enter bubbles. The amount of gas matters, but the route by which it leaves matters too.

Creating a bubble from nothing is not the easiest route. Tiny pre-existing gas pockets, including those held at small surface irregularities or particles, can provide starting places. The technical word is nucleation. In 2021 Gérard Liger-Belair and Clara Cilindre combined experiments on a commercial lager with physical modeling to examine these starting places and the possible number of bubbles in a glass. Their calculations depended on the beer, temperature, gas content and assumed geometry of the nucleation sites.[^c11-2]

The important discovery for our purposes is not a universal bubble count. There cannot be one without specifying the system being counted. It is that dissolved gas and the visible release of that gas are different observations. The same quantity in a liquid need not announce itself at the same pace under different surface conditions. A glass quietly streaming bubbles and a package abruptly overflowing may both involve carbon dioxide while presenting different physical problems.

Excess carbonation is one possible cause of excessive foaming. But brewing researchers distinguish a problem often called primary gushing, associated with substances carried from grain and malt. The surprising suspects are hydrophobins, small proteins made by filamentous fungi. Their name points toward an affinity for water-repelling surfaces, but their architecture lets them interact at boundaries between water and another phase. In the right circumstances, that surface behavior can have consequences much larger than their size suggests.

Tuija Sarlin and colleagues investigated gushing-active hydrophobins from *Fusarium graminearum* and related fungi. They identified candidate genes, produced selected proteins in another fungal host, purified them and tested their behavior. Their work, published online in 2011 and in a 2012 journal issue, reported vigorous gushing at very low concentrations of the purified proteins. This was direct experimental evidence that particular fungal proteins could induce the phenomenon.[^c11-3]

The route from a gene to an overflowing beer is an impressive piece of detective work. It moves beyond the observation that troublesome grain and troublesome foam sometimes occur together. Isolating an active substance helps distinguish the organism's many activities from the specific effect under investigation. The result does not require the fungus itself to be growing visibly in the bottle when it is opened. A material produced earlier can survive as the relevant participant.

How could the protein accomplish so much? Sylvie Deckers and colleagues proposed a mechanism involving very small gas structures stabilized by hydrophobins. Their 2011 research used dynamic light scattering, a method that infers information about small particles from fluctuations in scattered light. They observed approximately hundred-nanometer structures in the tested gushing beer and interpreted these as consistent with hydrophobin-stabilized nanobubbles. Extracts containing hydrophobin induced gushing in carbonated test beverages, while the still-water comparison did not gush.[^c11-4]

The contrast is revealing: an active protein is not an unlimited source of gas. The gas and the surface-active material have to participate together. At the same time, the instrument's particle-size signal is not a photograph of every proposed structure. The researchers' model offers a mechanism supported by their experiments; its components should retain the distinction between what was measured and how the measurement was interpreted.

At this scale, a bottle can contain an enormous amount of surface without containing an obvious visible object. That is one reason microscopic structures can matter to a macroscopic event. The dramatic rush of foam is the end of the story, when many small events become visible together. Judging solely from the size of the eruption encourages us to imagine a correspondingly large intruder. The experiments direct attention toward interfaces and tiny quantities instead.

Now return to the Canadian barley trials. Anuradha Jayathissa and colleagues had good reason to investigate *Fusarium*: it is an important grain pathogen, and its products can cause problems during malting and brewing. Yet their different measurements remained different. The authors discussed the possibility that other fungi, also capable of producing surface-active proteins, influenced the gushing tests. That was an explanation to investigate, rather than proof that those other organisms caused the result.[^c11-1]

The control grain helps expose a common misunderstanding of experiments. Uninoculated does not mean biologically empty. It means the researchers did not add the specified inoculum. Field-grown material can still encounter organisms and conditions outside that intervention. When a comparison surprises us, the temptation is to treat it as a nuisance. Here it prevents the elegant but unsupported equation of more measured *Fusarium* with more overflowing beer.

The toxin measurements belong to a separate investigation as well. A fungal species can make several substances, and the conditions that influence one output need not predict another. Detecting fungal DNA, measuring a toxin and observing foam are different analytical endpoints. The presence or absence of gushing cannot therefore replace the chemical measurements required to establish a toxin concentration. A quiet bottle is not a certificate; an erupting one is not a chemical identification.

Ordinary beer foam adds another layer. A brewer may want a stable head after pouring while emphatically not wanting liquid to escape from the package. Foam formation and foam persistence are related but distinct. Making a bubble requires one set of circumstances; keeping the films between bubbles from collapsing requires another. The word foamy can conceal the difference between gas release, ease of foam formation, durability and texture.

Chen Xu and colleagues investigated one of those relationships using protein Z, a barley-derived protein associated with beer foam, and humulinone, a hop-derived compound. Their laboratory study found that the interaction altered properties of the protein solution and improved its measured foamability and stability. This was work on a selected protein system, not evidence that every beer made with older hops would have a superior head.[^c11-5]

The experiment nevertheless earns a place in the gushing story. It demonstrates how incomplete the phrase just carbon dioxide is as an explanation of foam. The liquid's chemical constituents can influence what happens at its interfaces. To understand an eruption or a lasting head, researchers may have to investigate the gas, the surfaces and the compounds that occupy those surfaces. Their importance depends on the question, rather than on a fixed ranking of ingredients.

The practical detective work consequently begins by locating the event. Did foam rush from a package as it was opened? Did a poured glass lose its head unusually fast? Were bubbles attached to particular spots on the glass? These observations deserve different follow-up questions. Treating them all as too much or too little carbonation can hide the information needed to understand them. The spectacle is a starting point, however confidently it seems to announce its cause.

The trail can lead from that spectacle back through bottling, brewing, malting and farming. Yet it does not run backward automatically. Finding a plausible mechanism in a paper cannot diagnose an individual bottle any more than recognizing a footprint establishes the identity of every person who has walked across a field. The relevant tests must connect the mechanism to the actual materials and conditions.

What makes the case memorable is the scale of the connection. Grain can carry the consequences of interactions with fungi into a process designed around another fungus, yeast. Proteins associated with those earlier interactions can affect how gas behaves much later. The brewery is an impressive transformation, but it does not begin the story of everything it handles. Sometimes the foam remembers the field. The difficult part is determining what, exactly, it remembers.[^c11-6]
