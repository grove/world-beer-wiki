# 6. The Inventor Arrives Late

Samuel Whitbread bought a steam engine and kept the horses. The engine, ordered in 1784 and installed the following year at his London brewery, survives in the Powerhouse collection in Australia. Its gears and shafts drove equipment for crushing and moving malt, pumping, and stirring. Yet the brewery retained its horse wheel as insurance against mechanical failure. Modernization arrived with a backup plan.[^c06-1]

That practical arrangement is a useful entrance to the history of porter. A new machine could enter an existing brewery without creating the brewery, its market, or its beer. Its date of installation tells us something exact and important, but a great deal had already happened before someone signed the order. To begin the whole story with the engine would mistake one visible change for the system that made the change useful.

Porter's alleged invention presents a similar temptation. Here the memorable object is a man. In the familiar account, a London brewer named Harwood solves an irritating problem: publicans must draw three different drinks to produce a mixture called three-threads. He makes a single beer that supplies the combined taste from one cask. The result, associated with working porters, becomes porter. A chore disappears, a product appears, and the inventor has earned his place in history.

The trouble is the lateness of the complete story. Martyn Cornell's investigation traces its influential publication to John Feltham's *Picture of London* in 1802, decades after the supposed event. Cornell finds no contemporary support for the claim that porter was devised as a replacement for a three-cask mixture. Harwood was a real brewer; that does not make the narrated invention a documented occurrence.[^c06-2]

The distinction matters because a proper name gives an account an appearance of precision. We can picture a person conceiving an improvement more readily than we can picture an industry changing over several generations. The inventor story also flatters everyone involved. Customers know what they like, publicans struggle honorably to supply it, and a clever producer removes the inconvenience. The transaction contains no argument about prices, no expensive warehouse, and no year of waiting for payment. It is an admirably portable story about a less portable commodity.

To get closer to the commodity, open a brewing book published much nearer the beginning of the eighteenth century. The second edition of *The London and Country Brewer*, dated 1736 and attributed to William Ellis, describes brown, amber, and pale malts. Its account of London brewing has workers moving the mash with oars and judging heated water by observation and touch. Brewing already demands coordinated labor, equipment, judgment, and distinctions between materials. Nothing in that picture resembles a world awaiting the discovery that beer could be brown.[^c06-3]

The book's author is an argumentative guide. He wants readers to judge drinks and improve brewing, and his explanations mix practical observations with ideas about health that should remain firmly in their historical setting. His preface prefers an experienced eye to a fixed boiling time. His chapter on keeping beer considers how storage conditions influence the drink after it enters a cask. The brewery door is therefore no boundary between making and merely waiting: what happens afterward still helps determine what the customer receives.

That is a different kind of invention problem. Suppose a brewer discovers that customers prefer a product after a longer rest. Even without changing the principal ingredients, the brewer must change the business. Space becomes necessary. Containers remain occupied. Money spent on a batch cannot immediately pay for the next one. A desirable flavor may be inseparable from an undesirable delay in getting paid.

An imaginary calculation makes the constraint clear without pretending to reconstruct any particular firm's books. A brewery selling one batch every month, with each batch stored for one month, needs room for roughly a month's production at a time. If the same sales require twelve months of storage, it needs roughly twelve batches awaiting sale. Real brewing accounts are more complicated, but the direction is unavoidable. Increasing the age of a product increases the resources committed before it earns its return.

This is why the origins of porter cannot be settled by asking only what went into a kettle. A technique can be widely understood and still difficult to afford. Two brewers can possess comparable knowledge while only one has the premises, cash, and credit to act on it. A recipe that omits those conditions can explain how a drink is made while failing to explain who gets to make it commercially.

Historian James Sumner follows that larger transformation. His study finds substantial London brewers before porter's conventional invention date, and treats the drink's identity as something that developed alongside industrial concentration. He also follows the origin story through print: an account associated with the pseudonym Obadiah Poundage in 1760 was excerpted and circulated, while a more elaborate Harwood narrative appeared in 1802. Repetition eventually made borrowed accounts resemble independent agreement.[^c06-4]

One source copied into ten books remains one source for the copied claim. The ten books can, however, provide excellent evidence for something else: the success of the explanation. Its survival tells us that people wanted this sort of origin, and that publishers found it useful to supply one. A history of a drink and a history of what people say about the drink can occupy the same page while answering different questions.

This leaves a practical task for a reader following a footnote. A reference to an old book can identify evidence from the alleged inventor's lifetime, or a much later author repeating the familiar tale. Both books are old now. Their age relative to us is less useful than their distance from the event. The decisive date belongs beside the statement, and the statement's own source may lead to another book. What looks like a pile of testimony can turn out to be a single account traveling through a very long corridor.

Sumner's further argument concerns scale as a claim to authenticity. The great breweries' size became part of what made their porter seem special. His distinction is valuable: an enormous vessel can reduce costs or support a production method, but its impressive appearance can also persuade an observer that the product requires something unavailable elsewhere. Economic advantage becomes a story about the nature of the beer itself.

The argument should not flatten every producer into a calculating propagandist. A brewer might sincerely believe that a large vessel improved the drink. Customers might discern differences without understanding their causes. An explanation can be commercially convenient and honestly held at the same time. What needs testing is the step from an observation about one successful beer to a rule declaring that only a certain sort of establishment can produce the real thing.

Return now to Whitbread's engine. Unlike a moment of inspiration inside Harwood's head, it is a surviving object with a documented career. Even here, the material evidence needs interpretation. A photograph of a drawing, also held by the Powerhouse, preserves an inflated claim about the engine's horsepower. The museum's account explains that earlier figures of thirty-five horsepower, supposedly doubled to seventy, do not fit the other evidence. It gives a much smaller original output and a final rating around fifteen or sixteen horsepower.[^c06-5]

The important lesson is not that a museum caption defeats an old caption merely by being newer. The correction rests on comparing the claim with documents and the machine's physical characteristics. A confident number attached to an object is still a statement somebody made about it. The iron does not authenticate every sentence printed beneath its picture.

It also matters which date a description refers to. The engine was altered during its working life. Its later condition cannot simply be projected backward onto its arrival at the brewery. The same discipline applies to porter: a drink sold under an established name may change while the name continues. Continuity of the label cannot by itself establish continuity of color, ingredients, strength, aging, or the customer's understanding of what the label promises.

There is no need to replace the Harwood story with an equally tidy counterlegend in which one historian proves that no individual ever mattered. Brewing change depends on people trying things, deciding what to buy, persuading employers, managing failures, and teaching others. The absence of a secure single inventor does not dissolve their agency. It asks us to distribute attention more fairly among the kinds of work involved.

A porter history that includes storage makes room for the people who built and maintained containers. One that includes power makes room for the work the machine replaced, and the horses kept in reserve. One that includes publication makes room for writers whose explanations outlived the circumstances they described. These are connected histories; selecting only the most quotable one produces a narrower drink than the evidence supports.

Harwood can remain in the account as a brewer and as a figure in a remarkably successful origin story. He need not carry sole responsibility for a beer whose production and reputation changed over time. The more interesting question is how an industry came to be represented by one man's supposedly convenient idea. Once that question is asked, the late arrival of the inventor ceases to be an embarrassing gap. It becomes an event in its own right.[^c06-6]
