# 15. The Ingredient That Had to Be Carried

A twenty-nine-year-old pito brewer in Nagbo, northern Ghana, explained the problem in terms of the working week. When water was available, she could brew three or four times. When it was scarce, that might fall to once, or not at all. In the account published by Joseph Alhassan and colleagues in 2024, she connected the lost production to money needed for her household and her children's schooling.[^c15-1]

The difficulty was not a mysterious failure of fermentation. It began before fermentation could happen. The brewer knew how to make the drink; she could not reliably obtain one of the things that made the work possible.

A recipe would have written *water* on a line and moved on. Her account restores the distance between naming an ingredient and having it.

The study concerned women's livelihoods in the East and West Mamprusi municipalities. It combined a survey of 394 women with group discussions and other evidence. Pito brewing was one of the businesses discussed, alongside food preparation and trading. The Nagbo brewer's description was an individual account within that investigation, not the measured weekly output of all northern Ghanaian brewers. It is valuable because it shows how a shortage enters an ordinary decision: whether another batch can be made.

Once that decision is visible, a different kind of brewing history becomes possible. It follows the conditions of production through time, cash and work, instead of treating the brew vessel as the boundary of the subject. A missing supply can make every skill inside that boundary temporarily useless.

Consider the sequence implied by a commercial batch. Materials must be obtained before the drink can be sold. Work happens before revenue arrives. Containers and a place to serve need to be ready when customers want the product. If a producer cannot safely hold finished stock for long, selling time matters as much as making time. The precise arrangements vary; the ordering problem does not disappear merely because the enterprise is small.

That is why a questionnaire about pito can ask questions that would look quite ordinary in a study of any small business. In 2023, Aliata Issahaq Mumuni, Musah Dumah and Hawa Awuro Sam reported responses from 362 women brewers. Their methods describe purposive and convenience sampling in the Wa municipality, within the Upper West Region. The article foregrounds difficulties with finance and with packaging and storage. Its subject is an enterprise trying to keep operating, not an ornamental survival of a distant past.[^c15-2]

It would be easy to turn those findings into a heroic tale: resilient women overcome everything through determination. The problem with that tale is the word *everything*. A water supply cannot be replaced by an admirable personality. Nor does skill automatically provide affordable credit or a suitable container. Praising persistence can become a convenient way of looking away from the obstruction that required it.

The opposite story is no better: a supposedly backward technique waiting for someone else to arrive with modernity. It treats the producer's existing decisions as a blank space. A brewer deciding how much to make, when to buy materials and which customers to serve is already managing an uncertain business. An intervention should begin by understanding those decisions, including the constraints that make an apparently obvious change impractical.

There is a revealing distinction between buying equipment and financing a cycle of production. Equipment can remain in place while the business lacks money for its next batch. *Working capital* is the money needed to bridge that interval. A pot and a recipe do not fill the interval by themselves. Likewise, a larger vessel might increase potential output without increasing sales, available water or time. Capacity and livelihood are related, but they are not synonyms.

The Wa paper is not a reliable warrant for calculating a national number from its sample. Its tables contain reporting inconsistencies, and its statistical associations cannot establish that a particular constraint caused another. Those limitations matter when choosing how to use it. The descriptive finding that participants reported practical business difficulties is more defensible than a confident prescription based on a regression coefficient. The women deserve attention without having doubtful precision attached to their circumstances.

A separate 2024 paper from Wa discusses profits and sources of finance, but published figures from one local study should not be turned into the typical income of a Ghanaian brewer. A profit figure also needs a definition: which expenses were counted, over what period, and how was household labor treated? We cannot settle those questions by admiring the apparent exactness of a number.[^c15-3]

For a household enterprise, work can be economically consequential even when no wage changes hands. If the same person must collect water and prepare a batch, an extra hour spent on one task is unavailable for the other. If somebody else takes over the task, the cost has moved rather than vanished. This is why time belongs in an account of production alongside grain and fuel. Leaving it out can make a drink look cheaper to produce than its maker experiences it to be.

The Mamprusi study makes that connection concrete. Respondents described non-agricultural work as especially important when seasonal farming opportunities were limited. Water scarcity could then interfere with the activities on which they relied. That combination is more informative than treating brewing as a fixed occupation performed in identical conditions all year. A household's work changes with its opportunities; a shortage may arrive precisely when one source of income has become more necessary.

The geography must remain visible. Wa and the Mamprusi municipalities are not the same study location. Their participants are not one combined sample. The useful connection is a question crossing the studies: what must a woman secure, besides brewing knowledge, to turn that knowledge into income? Each investigation supplies a different portion of an answer.

To follow the drink beyond production, Isidore Lobnibe investigated pito bars and migration. His study, published in the 2018 volume of *African Geographical Review*, connects northern Ghana with the Brong Ahafo region, using fieldwork and the social life of two bars. The accessible abstract frames pito's movement through changes in agriculture, settlement and identity. A beverage can travel with people and become a way of understanding their changing surroundings.[^c15-4]

The view from a bar and the view from a brewing household need not coincide. A customer may value conversation, familiarity or a place to meet. The producer must also attend to what that visit costs and what it pays. A convincing history has room for both. Treating conviviality as the whole story risks making the work that sustains it disappear just as people begin enjoying themselves.

Nor should *pito* become a convenient replacement word for every West African cereal beer. Across the border, research on dolo in Burkina Faso describes women brewers often called *dolotières*, and an economy involving grain suppliers, malt preparation, fuel, transport and sales. A 2021 review led by Hagrétou Sawadogo-Lingani brings those connections together. It also identifies the difficulty of distributing a changing, short-lived product and the practical use of brewing by-products. This is a comparison of linked kinds of work, not a claim that the drinks or institutions are interchangeable.[^c15-5]

What the comparison offers is a wider cast. The person near the vessel may be the easiest participant to photograph, but the batch depends on people outside the frame. An account that stops at the pot is as incomplete economically as a chemical explanation that stops before fermentation. Grain has to arrive. Fuel has to arrive. The finished drink has to find its drinkers.

A proposal to change the process consequently has more than one outcome to consider. It might reduce fuel use but require a larger payment up front. It might extend storage but change the product customers expect. These are possible tradeoffs, not claims about a particular unexamined technology. They illustrate why the producer's own priorities belong at the center of the question. The most sophisticated apparatus is not automatically the most useful change.

The sources for this chapter were written by researchers; they are not interviews conducted for this book. They allow the Nagbo brewer's account and the reported concerns of other women to enter the narrative, but do not authorize a complete account of what every participant wanted. More direct reporting could ask which investment would make the largest difference to a particular enterprise, who would control it, and what new obligations it might introduce.[^c15-6]

For the brewer in Nagbo, however, one obstacle was already specific enough. Water availability changed how often she could work. No grand theory is needed to recognize the consequence of a batch that never begins.

The ingredients on a page can look reassuringly complete. Somewhere outside that page, someone still has to bring them home.
