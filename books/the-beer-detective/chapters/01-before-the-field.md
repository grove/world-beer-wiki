# 1. Before the Field

The object that might overturn your idea of a brewery is a hollow in a stone. There is no copper kettle, no chimney, no building with a sign above the door. At Raqefet Cave on Mount Carmel, in present-day Israel, researchers examined three mortars associated with a Natufian burial site. In 2018, Li Liu and her colleagues proposed that some of the traces preserved there came from brewing roughly 13,000 years ago. If their interpretation is right, people were making a cereal-based alcoholic drink before the region's domesticated cereals appeared.[^c01-1]

It is a finding with an irresistible sequel. First there was beer; then someone decided to plant enough grain to keep the beer coming; then came farms, villages, cities and, eventually, the person who asks you to fill in a form. Civilization as the unintended consequence of a drinks order.

The stone does not tell that story. It poses a better question: what would count as evidence that someone brewed before there were farmers?

We need to begin with the grain. A wild cereal already contains starch. Domestication did not invent this useful material, and a person did not need to own a field to collect it. The difficulty for a brewer is that starch is a store of linked sugar units, whereas ordinary brewing yeast needs sugars it can take up and use. A pile of grain and some water are therefore the beginning of a problem, not a complete solution. The potential alcohol is locked away.

Germination offers one way in. A sprouting seed must mobilize its food reserve to support its own growth. Enzymes help break the reserve into smaller, usable molecules. A brewer who moistens grain, lets it germinate and then manages what happens next is borrowing a plant's machinery. Heating a mixture of crushed grain and water can make starch more accessible; under suitable conditions, enzymes continue the conversion. Yeast can then turn available sugars into alcohol and carbon dioxide. These are distinguishable operations, although no early brewer needed to name an enzyme to discover a useful sequence.[^c01-2]

This distinction matters archaeologically. Evidence that people possessed grain is not evidence that they brewed it. Evidence that they ground grain is not enough either. Grinding can lead toward bread, porridge or other foods. Even a vessel suited to holding liquid gives us a range of possibilities. To identify brewing, researchers must find something more discriminating than a plausible container and an appetite.

The Raqefet investigation combined the wear on stone surfaces, microscopic plant residues and experiments in processing cereals. The team identified a range of plant materials, including wheat or barley, and interpreted two of the mortars as storage facilities and another as a place for processing food, including beer. Experimental comparisons helped them ask whether the ancient starch had suffered changes consistent with malting, heating and fermentation. This was an argument built from several kinds of trace, rather than the discovery of ancient liquid.[^c01-1]

A starch granule is small, but it has a structure. Processing can pit it, distort it or destroy part of it. The principle resembles the reading of a damaged tool: the damage may preserve information about the forces that acted on it. Yet a mark is useful only if we understand how distinctive it is. A scratch on a door establishes contact with something hard. It does not, by itself, identify a burglar. The scientific work lies in comparing the mark with alternatives, including uneventful ones.

That is where this case became a dispute. In a published response in 2019, Liu and colleagues addressed objections raised by David Eitam about mortar functions, starch damage, vessel size and the proposed heating method. They defended their interpretation as a convergence of evidence and emphasized that they were not assigning a brewing function to every Natufian mortar. The exchange matters because an ancient beer claim can sound conclusive once detached from the discussion that followed it. Here the discussion remained technical and consequential: how do we distinguish deliberate brewing from other changes to grain?[^c01-3]

The disagreement does not oblige us to dismiss the find. Nor can we resolve it by voting for the more appealing account. It tells us where the weight rests. If several different processes can produce a particular feature, that feature must be read with its companions. If an experimental procedure produces a similar residue, the experiment demonstrates a possible pathway. Additional evidence is needed to show that this was the pathway taken at the site. A successful reconstruction is a valuable result; it is not a recording.

Then there is the setting. Raqefet was a place where people buried their dead. A separate study, published in 2013 by Dani Nadel and colleagues, reported plant impressions interpreted as linings in four Natufian graves, including impressions identified as flowering stems. The research concerns the preparation of graves, not beer. Its relevance is that the cave preserves deliberate activities around burial. The brewing researchers' proposal of drink served at ritual feasts belongs within this context, but a burial ground cannot tell us who drank, what words were spoken or how grief felt.[^c01-4]

Suppose, for the moment, that the brewing interpretation proves correct. Have we established that beer came before agriculture? We have made progress, but the question still needs better wording. Collecting wild plants, deliberately cultivating them, and depending on domesticated crops are different activities. They need not begin together. The history of agriculture is not a switch with a hunter-gatherer position on the left and a farmer position on the right.

In 2006, Ken-ichi Tanno and George Willcox examined archaeological wheat remains and argued that domestication was slower than some previous estimates suggested. Ears that retained their seeds, a useful property for harvesting, appeared gradually; wild forms persisted in cultivated fields. Their study concerned wheat domestication, not an alcoholic motive for it. It helps explain why a phrase such as “before farming” can conceal several different chronological claims.[^c01-5]

A person can work hard to obtain wild grain. Another can sow grain whose inherited characteristics still resemble those of wild populations. A community can plant some food while continuing to gather and hunt for much of the rest. A brewing discovery must be placed within that changing landscape of practices. It cannot be made to stand on one side of a sharp boundary simply because the boundary is useful for a headline.

There is also a rival in the supposed contest for the first use of grain. In 2018, Amaia Arranz-Otaegui and colleagues reported charred bread-like remains from Shubayqa 1 in northeastern Jordan, dating to about 14,400 years ago. Their analyses identified evidence of prepared foods using wild cereals and other plant materials, well before an agricultural way of life. The oldest surviving evidence for a food is not its birth certificate, but these remains make a cereal world with several possibilities more credible than a single-file procession from beer to bread, or bread to beer.[^c01-6]

The usual argument asks which product deserves credit for agriculture. Another way to approach it is to notice what both require: knowledge of plants, repeated handling and an interest in turning something difficult to eat into something worth making. Several foods might encourage attention to the same grain. A favored drink could matter without being the sole cause of a transformed food system. Causation is not a prize that only one ingredient can win.

To prove that brewing drove cultivation, we would need evidence connecting choices over time: grain acquired or grown for that purpose, repeated investment, consequences that distinguish the brewing explanation from other explanations. A single early brewing site, however persuasive, cannot supply the whole sequence. Earlier in time does not mean responsible for what followed. The first person in a queue has not necessarily summoned everyone else.

The most interesting result at Raqefet survives this subtraction. A society without domesticated cereal fields may have possessed intricate knowledge of how grain changes. The people responsible would not have been waiting for a later civilization to teach them technology. If the mortars record brewing, they record practical accomplishment in its own setting, with purposes we can only partly recover. If the interpretation changes, the work of comparing residues, tools and experiments will still have made the question more precise.[^c01-7]

For now, we can leave the grand procession of civilization outside the cave. Inside, there are three studied mortars, microscopic traces, an argument and a much larger collection of unexamined possibilities. The gap between those things and a confident creation story is not empty. It is where the next investigation belongs.
