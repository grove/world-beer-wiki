# 4. What the Pottery Remembered

In 2004, a team studying pottery from Jiahu, a Neolithic site in China's Henan province, proposed a drink made with rice, honey and fruit. In 2026, another team returned to the site's brewing problem and gave a prominent role to molds. The first account names things you might put in a pot. The second asks what could help transform them. Both belong to the same investigation.[^c04-1][^c04-2]

The apparent change is less about replacing one ingredient list with another than about asking what makes an ingredient list work. Rice, honey and fruit can name materials without explaining the transformations that put them into a drink. A molecule surviving in pottery and the remains of a microscopic organism answer different questions. Jiahu is an unusually good place to watch those answers meet.

The 2004 research, led by Patrick McGovern with Chinese and other international collaborators, examined organic residues absorbed into ancient pottery. Its argument combined chemical results with archaeological and botanical context. It proposed fruit that could include hawthorn or grape, rather than identifying a single fruit beyond doubt. Crucially, the researchers had not detected surviving ethanol. They inferred fermentation from the combination of ingredients and their properties.[^c04-1]

That last distinction deserves to be kept in view. Ancient drink is commonly reported as though someone has uncorked a bottle and sent its contents to a laboratory. Jiahu's pottery offered traces of what had contacted or penetrated its surfaces. The drink itself had disappeared. An absence of ethanol in material this old is hardly surprising, but it means the scientific argument must be reconstructed from other evidence. We are examining what a container retained, not tasting what a container held.

The chemical approach is a kind of comparison. A sample produces signals; researchers ask which substances can account for them. Different methods can separate components or reveal features of their molecular structure. Candidate ingredients must fit the signals and the archaeological setting. Agreement across methods strengthens an interpretation, while a signal shared by several possible sources demands more work. Chemistry can narrow the field remarkably without giving every molecule a unique postal address.

The early report illustrates the problem. Tartaric acid helped raise the possibility of fruit, but the authors discussed more than one possible source. Wax-like compounds supported an argument involving honey while leaving room for chemically similar plant materials. These qualifications were present in the research, even though “rice, honey and fruit” was the version easiest to remember. A list is portable. The reasoning behind a list is heavier luggage.[^c04-1]

The public impact was immediate enough to be dated. The University of Pennsylvania announced the findings on 8 December 2004. Its account also described much later liquids preserved in tightly lidded bronze vessels. Those liquids were a separate set of evidence, separated from Neolithic Jiahu by thousands of years. Put both discoveries into one short story and it becomes easy to transfer the wonderful survival of liquid in bronze to the much older pottery. The actual materials require us to keep the investigations apart.[^c04-3]

Now consider what the three proposed ingredients imply. Honey and fruit can supply fermentable sugars directly. Rice is rich in starch. To turn much of that starch into alcohol, a brewing process needs an effective way to break it down. Naming the grain leaves a biochemical question unanswered. This is where microscopic organisms become more than accidental guests.

Some molds produce enzymes that convert starch into smaller sugars. Yeasts can then use suitable sugars in fermentation. The tasks need not be performed by one organism. Nor does starch conversion have to depend exclusively on sprouting the grain, the route familiar from malt. A starter used in a *qu*-based process can carry organisms that perform complementary work. In such a system, mold is part of the proposed technology. Treating every mold as evidence that food has gone wrong would prevent us from understanding how the food was made.[^c04-2]

This does not mean that any growth on grain is desirable, or that an archaeological interpretation provides instructions for selecting a safe starter. It means that the category “microbe” is much too broad to explain a role. The useful question is what a particular community can do under particular conditions. A drink's ingredient list may therefore leave out some of its most consequential participants, even when those participants are doing much of the chemical work.

Li Liu and colleagues' 2026 Jiahu study examined microfossils in sixteen pottery sherds. They reported starch, plant silica bodies called phytoliths, and fungal remains, including structures assigned to *Monascus*. The combined evidence led them to propose predominantly mold-assisted starch conversion, with signs compatible with malt-based processing as well. They did not require honey and fruit to disappear from the picture; those materials could still have supplied additional sugars.[^c04-2]

At first glance, a microscopic shape seems more tangible than a chemical signal. Here is something that once had a structure. Yet identification has its own demands. A damaged granule can lose the features that distinguish one plant from another. A cell's outline may resemble that of a familiar yeast without establishing its species. Recognizing a possible organism is also different from reconstructing every member of an ancient fermentation community. A few surviving forms cannot give us a complete roster.

The newer paper makes a further boundary explicit: it did not perform a parallel modern chemical reanalysis of the original pottery samples. It offers a complementary line of inquiry. We should therefore avoid describing the research as two laboratories testing one identical ancient portion and producing contradictory results. The shared point is the site and the historical problem. The samples, methods and questions must still be specified.[^c04-2]

Two other Chinese investigations help explain the value of the additional approach. In work published in 2016, Jiajing Wang and colleagues studied residues from pottery associated with pits at Mijiaya, in Shaanxi. Their evidence included starch, phytoliths and chemistry, together with equipment they interpreted as a brewing assemblage. They proposed a combination of cereals and other starchy plants. This was a later site, around five thousand years old, with its own materials and context. Its relevance here is methodological: an interpretation can draw on the arrangement of equipment as well as on what survived inside it.[^c04-4]

At Qiaotou in southern China, research published in 2021 examined pottery from a platform mound associated with burials. The team used microfossil evidence to propose fermented drinks involving rice and other plant foods, with mold-assisted processing. Again, this was neither Jiahu nor a duplicate of it. It showed why a researcher returning to Jiahu might ask questions that a chemical ingredient list had not fully answered. Neighboring problems can suggest a new examination without dictating its result.[^c04-5]

There is a risk in lining up these finds on a map. The line connecting points can start to look like a route, and the route can become a story of invention and transmission. Similar evidence might indeed support historical connections, but shared materials or processes do not demonstrate who learned from whom. Establishing movement needs chronology, geography and further evidence. An analytical resemblance is a clue to investigate, not a family tree already drawn.

Even within one site, the contents of a pot can be harder to separate than they first appear. An object may have been reused. Materials could have reached it at different times. A vessel involved in brewing might later have held a food containing fermentation residue. These are possibilities to evaluate against the context, not reasons to assume that every result is contamination. The container preserves an accumulation; the researcher is trying to recover events.

One useful test is to ask how the account would change if an ingredient vanished from it. Suppose future work weakened the evidence for honey. That would matter to the proposed mixture, but it would not automatically erase every indication of cereal processing. Suppose a particular fungal identification were revised. That would affect a proposed mechanism, while leaving other plant traces available for study. Claims should be connected closely enough to support an argument but not fused so tightly that the correction of one requires burning down the whole account.

Jiahu's importance is not diminished by gaining this more complicated history of analysis. The research has moved from identifying a plausible combination of materials toward investigating how some of those materials became available for fermentation. The pottery has not changed its story out of malice. The questions have become more specific, and the surviving traces have been asked to do different work.[^c04-6]

What emerges is a less familiar image of a brewery. Its decisive machinery need not be a large, visible object. Grain can be transformed by organisms working alongside one another, with human practice arranging the conditions. The most consequential part of the operation may once have been too small for its makers to see. Thousands of years later, it has become part of what makes their skill visible to us.
