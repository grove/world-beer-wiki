# 5. The Last Service

The drinking vessels were broken. The building had burned. Yet the excavators of the brewery at Cerro Baúl, in southern Peru, did not interpret this combination as an ordinary accident or a sudden attack. They proposed an organized ending: brewing, feasting and drinking, followed by deliberate destruction as the inhabitants withdrew. A place made to bring people together had been taken apart in a final act of gathering.[^c05-1]

It is a striking way to close a brewery. It is also a difficult one to investigate. Ash can establish a fire without identifying the person who lit it. A broken cup can record a fall, a blow or an act of deliberate sacrifice. The interpretation has to come from the arrangement and sequence of the remains. This case begins with destruction and works backward toward the institution that was worth destroying.

Cerro Baúl is a mesa in the Moquegua region. During the first millennium CE it became a Wari center, near communities associated with the rival Tiwanaku polity. These were states that preceded the Inca; calling the site an Inca brewery would collapse distinct histories. Michael Moseley and his colleagues described the settlement as serving an embassy-like delegation, with monumental buildings and the agricultural support needed to maintain occupation. Their account places the brewery within a long-established frontier enclave, rather than treating it as an isolated place that happened to make a great deal of drink.[^c05-1]

The distinction changes the question. A large brewery at a political center can do more than supply a thirst that already exists. The occasions on which its product is served can help define relationships. Who hosts, who is invited, where people gather and which vessels they use can all be part of the transaction. The drink has to be understood alongside the setting that makes its distribution significant.

First, however, someone has to make it. The excavated building contained separate spaces interpreted as areas for milling, heating and fermentation, together with hearths, grinding equipment and large ceramic vessels. The original researchers estimated a capacity of roughly 1,800 liters per batch. That figure is an archaeological reconstruction from the installation and vessels, not a surviving record of what every batch actually yielded.[^c05-1]

Even as an estimate, it forces a change of scale. Water weighs about a kilogram per liter. A volume in that range represents close to two tonnes of water before we consider ingredients, fuel and containers. The position of the brewery on a summit makes the apparently simple statement “beer was served” conceal a considerable amount of movement. A feast's spectacle depended on work that began before its guests arrived.

Archaeologist Donna Nash has emphasized this labor in explaining the site and its experimental investigation. Her account describes bringing water and materials up to the brewery, and the practical effort involved in producing chicha. She also points to *tupu* pins, associated with women's dress, among the archaeological evidence used to infer the involvement of elite women in brewing. These are clues about the people at work, not a list of names or a set of eyewitness portraits.[^c05-2]

That distinction allows us to recognize skilled women without supplying them with invented biographies. We cannot say what a particular brewer thought about the people she served. We can say that the published interpretation puts high-status women within a demanding productive activity. Rank and manual competence are not opposites. A society may attach importance to who is entitled to make a valued thing, as well as to who is entitled to receive it.

The drink itself has another surprise. *Chicha* is a name applied to diverse fermented beverages, and the Cerro Baúl evidence does not reduce neatly to maize alone. Molle, the Peruvian pepper tree, matters here. Its small fruits can provide sugars for fermentation. The archaeological and experimental work has considered maize preparations, molle preparations and mixtures of the two. The proportions and exact practices of every ancient batch remain beyond recovery.[^c05-3]

For a reader used to barley and hops, the fruit can look like an exotic addition. That is a poor starting point. The relevant question is what locally available materials enabled brewers to do. A sugar-bearing fruit and a starchy grain present different processing problems. Molle need not be understood as a flavoring added to a presumed European beer. It can be central to the beverage and to the knowledge required to make it.

Research published in 2019 by Patrick Ryan Williams, Nash, Joshua Henkin and Ruth Ann Armitage examined both the ceramics and the traces within them. The team found evidence consistent with molle in the pores of brewing and serving vessels and argued for regional sources of ceramic raw materials. This brought two supply questions into one account: where the containers came from and what went into them. The authors connected local resources and a drought-tolerant ingredient with the resilience of the feasting institution.[^c05-4]

The important object is therefore not just a cup, considered as a finished piece of pottery. It belongs to a chain of decisions about clay, manufacture, imagery, contents and use. If replacements can be made from materials available in the region, the system depends less on uninterrupted shipments from a distant center. If a beverage can be produced from plants suited to the local environment, that may help the institution continue through some kinds of stress. These are practical advantages, not a guarantee that political relationships will survive indefinitely.

The popular shorthand is that beer held an empire together. It has the convenience of fitting on a label. The research asks a narrower and more interesting question: how could repeated occasions of making and sharing a particular drink help people enact political relationships? A gathering can be hospitable and unequal at the same time. Receiving something from a powerful host need not make the recipient powerless, but the circumstances of the gift can matter. A feast is an event whose social meaning is not measured by its volume alone.

This makes the closing act more legible. If brewing and serving helped sustain the enclave's relations, destroying the facility and its vessels could mark an ending to that arrangement. The excavators' ceremonial-closure interpretation draws force from the broader pattern of evacuation and the particular treatment of objects. We can follow that reasoning without claiming to know the exact belief or intention of each participant. Deliberate action can be recognizable even when its full explanation is not.

The fire also changes what survives for archaeology. An operating brewery is a place where containers are moved, repaired and replaced. The assemblage left at closure may be unusual precisely because the ending was unusual. It offers a concentrated view of equipment and objects, while inviting the mistake of treating a final episode as a normal working day. The most vivid evidence is not necessarily the most representative evidence.

Later research pushed the question beyond the monumental site. In a study published in 2024, Nash, Matthew Biwer and Emilee Witte compared excavated deposits with experiments guided by knowledgeable brewers. They considered what residues different chicha preparations leave and argued that, in the Wari-affiliated settings they examined in Moquegua, production was largely specialized and state-supported. The abstract does not support a blanket conclusion that households throughout the Wari world never brewed. It challenges an assumption of universal domestic production in a defined place and period.[^c05-3]

Experiments of this kind depend on contemporary knowledge. A brewer who knows how to handle a fruit, judge a mixture or separate useful liquid from residue is contributing expertise that a laboratory instrument does not replace. At the same time, present-day practice should not be treated as an unchanged recording of the first millennium. Its value is practical and comparative. The people involved are collaborators in solving a problem, not exhibits demonstrating an ancient past.

There is a productive tension between the palace-scale view and the residue-scale view. One asks how a political institution operated. The other asks what an activity leaves behind. If we cannot recognize small-scale brewing traces, we may wrongly conclude that only great breweries existed. If we assume that every plausible pot held beer, we may invent a domestic industry. Good comparisons help specify what should be visible under each possibility, and how much may have escaped preservation or recognition.

Cerro Baúl has also inspired modern beer. The Field Museum described its collaboration with Off Color Brewing on a drink using purple maize and molle. The museum's account says that the brewers developed and selected among recipes. This is a legitimate way to explore and communicate an archaeological subject, provided “inspired by” keeps its meaning. A contemporary glass can invite interest in the site without posing as a sample of its last batch.[^c05-5]

The final service at the ancient brewery cannot be repeated. We have no guest list for the closing feast, no speech announcing departure, no agreed explanation for every object left behind. What we have is more material: an installation built for substantial work, vessels and ingredients linked to a political institution, and a destructive ending that the excavators interpret as planned.[^c05-6]

To destroy an ordinary cup is to lose a useful object. To destroy a cup that helps enact an affiliation may also be to terminate a relationship. At Cerro Baúl, the broken vessels make the institution visible from its far end. We meet the brewery at the moment it stopped being available for another gathering.
