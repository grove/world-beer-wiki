# 9. The Culprit Was Light

The decisive witness in one beer investigation was a particle that would never survive long enough to be bottled. In a 2001 experiment, Colin Burns and colleagues used laser pulses and electron paramagnetic resonance to examine what happened when light struck hop-derived bittering compounds. Their subject was the route toward an aroma politely described as lightstruck and less politely described as skunky. The apparatus was listening, in effect, for the fleeting chemistry between the beer as made and the beer as smelled.[^c09-1]

There was no skunk in the experiment. There need not be one in the explanation. The animal lends its name to a sensory resemblance, just as banana does in a wheat beer. But this case differs from the banana case in a crucial respect. A desirable aroma may be produced during a planned fermentation. Here, an exposure after brewing can create a smell that the brewer did not intend. The ingredient list is unchanged while the contents cease to be quite the same.

The best-known offender has an uninviting name: 3-methyl-2-butene-1-thiol, usually shortened to MBT. The thiol part identifies a sulfur-containing chemical group. Small amounts can have a conspicuous sensory effect. A 1993 study by A. J. Irwin, L. Bordeleau and R. L. Barker estimated a threshold of seven nanograms per liter in the beer and test conditions they used. A nanogram is a billionth of a gram. This is a drama in which the central character occupies very little space.[^c09-2]

That threshold is a measured result, not an immutable boundary built into every nose and every beer. It nevertheless explains why the damage can be disproportionate to the quantity formed. A beer does not need to turn visibly cloudy, change color or lose a measurable portion of its bulk before a new odor becomes noticeable. The sensory world has no obligation to allocate attention in proportion to mass.

To understand how MBT appears, start with a useful ingredient. Hop-derived iso-alpha acids contribute bitterness. Their structures also contain portions capable of taking part in photochemical breakdown. Light is energy, and a molecule that absorbs it may enter an excited state from which reactions become possible. The relevant question is therefore not simply whether a bottle has become warm. A beam of light can do chemical work that a thermometer fails to describe.

Burns's team examined a direct light-driven pathway in a model solvent system. Their laser operated at a specific ultraviolet wavelength, and the researchers analyzed short-lived radicals, reactive intermediates with unpaired electrons. The method helped distinguish where energy was absorbed from where a bond subsequently broke. Their findings were a way into the mechanism, not a simulation of every bottle on every shop shelf.[^c09-1]

The distinction between a model and a bottle is where the next piece enters. Beer contains riboflavin, also known as vitamin B2, which can absorb light and participate in reactions involving other molecules. A substance familiar from nutrition becomes a participant in flavor damage. Chemistry is indifferent to whether we have placed a compound in the good column of a dietary chart. Its behavior depends on the company it keeps and the energy it encounters.

In research published in 2005, Kevin Huvaere and colleagues investigated interactions between light-excited riboflavin and hop bittering compounds. They trapped reactive intermediates and examined their signals, supporting a pathway from isohumulones toward precursors of MBT. Reduced hop derivatives behaved differently; their reactions could generate precursors associated with other flavor changes. Preventing one particular route was not equivalent to granting the beer immunity from all photochemistry.[^c09-3]

The missing sulfur had to be accounted for as well. In a 2006 paper, Huvaere and colleagues followed the light-driven degradation of sulfur-containing amino acids and related substances in the presence of flavins. They combined kinetic observations, radical detection and identification of reaction products. Their findings connected sulfur-bearing intermediates with a carbon-containing fragment derived from the hop compounds, supplying the pieces needed for the characteristic lightstruck molecule.[^c09-4]

The light did not have to be ultraviolet. Visible light drove the relevant chemistry in the flavin experiments. This widens the question a protective package must answer. Saying that a material blocks ultraviolet rays does not describe everything it lets through. The useful boundary is drawn by the molecules' response to the transmitted light, rather than by a familiar division in the spectrum.[^c09-4]

The explanation is more satisfying than the vague idea that sunlight makes beer go bad. It specifies materials already present, energy arriving from outside and a pathway by which new molecules can form. It also shows why an unopened package is not necessarily a protected package. A seal controls the passage of material through a closure; transparent walls admit energy without opening a hole. Nothing needs to crawl under the cap.

Following the mechanism into finished beer brings a practical question: what can investigators measure in the more crowded liquid as light exposure proceeds?

Richard Pozdrik and colleagues took a useful step toward the actual drink in 2006. They examined mainstream lager and model beer, tracking the disappearance of riboflavin's absorbance at 445 nanometers during light exposure. That change was associated with the development of lightstruck character. Several tested additions reduced the changes but did not stop them. In the model system, increasing isohumulone could diminish the protective effect of some additions.[^c09-5]

There is a modest but important lesson in that last complication. Adding a substance called an antioxidant does not automatically stop every undesirable reaction under every condition. A brewer needs an effect demonstrated in the relevant formulation, exposure and sensory outcome. The experiment offers evidence of interacting variables, not a kitchen remedy. A promising ingredient cannot be promoted into a guarantee merely because its general chemical description sounds reassuring.

The bottle then becomes part of the investigation. It is tempting to arrange glass colors into a simple league table and finish the chapter there. Amber is usually a sensible clue, but a color name conceals a spectrum. Two objects described as green may transmit rather different amounts of light at the wavelengths that matter. Thickness also changes the route light takes through the material. The apparent color is what our eyes receive, not a complete report on what the contents receive.

Barry Hucker and colleagues measured transmission through bottle glass of several colors and thicknesses in work published in 2016. Their amber samples strongly restricted relevant light, while clear and bright green glass transmitted more. Darker green glass behaved differently from bright green. Within the amber group, thicker glass obstructed more light than thinner glass. These measurements make a more useful argument for protection than the claim that every bottle of one color must behave identically.[^c09-6]

They also defeat a small piece of bottle folklore. Green glass cannot tell you how old a beer is. It gives information about a container, and even that information is incomplete without its actual transmission properties. A bottle kept in darkness and a bottle placed under light may look alike before opening. Their exposure histories can differ. Age and light damage are separate variables, although real storage may combine them.

From this perspective, the package is neither an innocent wrapper nor a universal villain. It mediates an encounter between the surroundings and the drink. That is true even when its surface advertises a tradition or displays an attractive color. The same object can perform a cultural job, a commercial job and a physical job, with no requirement that those jobs agree. A beautiful view of the beer may be exactly the route by which unwanted light reaches it.

This does not mean that every sulfurous smell is MBT, or that a familiar aromatic impression proves one mechanism without further evidence. It means that a particular explanation has earned its place through experiments. If the smell is suspected to be lightstruck, the exposure, formulation and analytical result matter. If it is ordinary aging, another investigation is needed. Placing everything under a single heading such as stale would hide the useful differences.

The glass measurements complete the route from the surroundings to the smell. Exposure supplies energy; the package controls how much reaches the beer; molecules inside participate in reactions; a small amount of their product can become perceptible. Each link gives investigators something specific to test when a bottle develops the suspected aroma.

The cap, meanwhile, can remain perfectly tight. That is what gives this case its pleasingly unfair conclusion. The brewer may have guarded the liquid against a host of material intruders while the culprit entered through the walls, carrying no substance of its own. It was light, doing what light does when an accommodating molecule is waiting on the other side.[^c09-7]
