# 17. The Cake That Would Not Be a Strain

The yeast appears to have gone missing.

In a survey published in 2013, researchers examined forty-two samples of nuruk, the Korean fermentation starter. They recovered *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, the familiar alcoholic fermenter, from eighteen. Two years later, a study following makgeolli through fermentation reported that *Saccharomyces* dominated the fungal community. Here, apparently, was an organism absent from more than half the starters yet overwhelming in the drink. Before invoking a mystery of microbial succession, however, there is a less glamorous place to look: the second paper's methods.[^c17-1]

The investigators had added commercial yeast.

Their experiment used nuruk and a separate commercial yeast starter together. It was a legitimate study of how that particular fermentation developed. It was not an experiment showing what every nuruk cake would do unaided. Two accurate results had become an apparent contradiction because the experiments were being asked to answer the same question. They had never agreed to do that.[^c17-2]

This is an unusually useful mistake to catch. Beer writing often treats the word *starter* as if it meant a packet containing one selected strain. Nuruk makes that assumption awkward. The cake carries a material, a history of cultivation, enzymes, and a collection of organisms. Asking which yeast is inside it is sensible. Treating the answer as a complete description of what it does is much less so.

Start with the grain. Much of the carbohydrate in cereal is starch, an arrangement of linked sugar units. Alcoholic fermentation requires accessible sugars, not merely the promise of sugars bound into larger structures. A fermentation system therefore needs a way to cut those structures apart. It also needs organisms that can turn the resulting food into alcohol. The two jobs can overlap in time without being identical. A single label on a starter obscures that division of work.

The easy explanation assigns starch conversion to molds and alcohol to yeasts. It is a useful first sketch and a poor border fence. In 2016 Jin Ho Choo and colleagues investigated two *Saccharomycopsis fibuligera* isolates obtained from wheat-based nuruk, one from Jeju and one from Pohang. They selected the isolates for strong saccharification activity: these yeasts were good at helping release sugars from starch. Their genomic and physiological work examined an organism whose contribution could not be reduced to the job usually assigned to yeast in a brewing cartoon.[^c17-3]

For the brewer, functional cooperation need not follow our categories. An organism can matter because it prepares food for another organism. Its importance might be poorly represented by how much of it remains when someone samples the finished drink. The enzyme it produced earlier and the cell counted later are different objects of measurement. That distinction is elementary, but it changes what a laboratory list can tell us about a cake.

Return to the forty-two samples. Song and colleagues also recovered the mold *Aspergillus oryzae* from thirteen. Neither number licenses a declaration that the other cakes contained no useful mold or yeast. Isolation is an encounter between a sample and a method. Organisms must survive, grow under the chosen conditions, and be recognized. A failure to recover one species is not equivalent to proving that no cell of it was present. Nor does its recovery establish that it performed most of the work during a later brew.

A good detective asks how the witness came into the room. In this case the witness is a colony on a laboratory medium. The conditions that made it visible are part of the testimony.

The makgeolli time course asks a different question. Its researchers followed a controlled batch through successive days, measuring a changing fermentation rather than comparing many dry starters at one moment. Bacterial composition changed, especially over the first two days. The fungal community showed no significant change, and *Saccharomyces* remained dominant throughout. This was not a record of that yeast emerging from obscurity to displace its rivals. Its presence from the start makes the additional inoculation central to interpreting the result.

The attractive but unsupported version of the story would say that a diverse traditional cake naturally sorts itself until the familiar brewing yeast takes command. That may be a hypothesis to examine in an appropriate experiment. It is not the conclusion supplied by this particular comparison. The missing ingredient in our interpretation was printed in the method, available before any speculation began.

The next cake takes us to another set of places and another research question. Pooja Pradhan and Jyoti Prakash Tamang studied dry fermentation starters from the eastern Himalayas in work published in 2019. Their thirty-five samples included starters called marcha from Nepal, Darjeeling, Sikkim, and Bhutan, alongside separately named starters from Arunachal Pradesh and Bhutan. They obtained 201 bacterial isolates and selected sixty-eight representatives for genetic identification. This was an investigation of recoverable bacteria, not an inventory of every yeast, mold, and bacterium inhabiting every Himalayan starter.[^c17-4]

The distinction between a starter and a strain is especially clear in their account of making new cakes using material from an earlier starter. The practice passes something biological forward, but it also passes a technique: what material to prepare, how to incorporate the inoculum, when to dry it, and how to judge the result. A cake is both an ingredient for a future fermentation and evidence of work already done.

There is no reason to assume that this transmission preserves every organism at a fixed proportion. Each new preparation presents conditions in which some organisms may grow more readily than others. Drying and later use introduce further transitions. Continuity can therefore reside in a maintained practice without requiring an unchanging microbial membership. The human task is not made less skilled because the system is dynamic. Working with a changing material may be precisely what demands judgment.

Nor do the shared laboratory questions turn the named starters into interchangeable examples of one recipe. A researcher can investigate bacteria from marcha and phab in the same study while retaining the names, collection places, and boundaries of the samples. A reader should be able to do the same. A common analytical instrument does not create a common cultural identity.

This creates a problem for preservation. Imagine, as a thought experiment, conserving a single isolate from a starter. The culture may be scientifically valuable. It may make a repeatable product. But what exactly has been saved? One organism has been preserved. The relationships among organisms may not have been. The grain preparation has not necessarily been taught, and the reasons a maker accepts one cake and rejects another have not automatically entered the freezer.

Now imagine preserving only a written recipe. Quantities and operations survive, but the material may respond differently when ingredients, humidity, equipment, or available inoculum change. A recipe can guide practice without exhaustively containing it. Neither of these imagined preservation efforts is useless. Each simply preserves a different portion of the thing we were calling a starter.

A public heritage document recognizes a wider frame. In 2021 Korea's Cultural Heritage Administration described the national designation of makgeolli making and sharing. Its account includes preparation and social practices of offering and sharing the beverage. That designation does not establish a microbial specification for nuruk, and it cannot speak for every maker's preferred technique. It does make visible a unit of continuity larger than a species name: the acts through which people produce a drink and bring it into relationships.[^c17-5]

There is a temptation to make this sound mystical, as if acknowledging knowledge beyond a recipe required surrendering to the unknowable. Nothing in the evidence requires that move. Temperatures can be measured. Organisms can be isolated and sequenced. Enzyme activity can be tested. Makers can explain choices, disagree with one another, and revise procedures. What changes is the scale of the inquiry. A chemical measurement and a conversation about acceptable results would answer related questions without becoming substitutes for each other.

The next useful study would not begin by announcing that the cake had finally been decoded. It would state what had been collected, who made it, what question the makers considered important, and which transformations the measurements followed. Those details would make a result more portable because they would show the conditions under which it was obtained. They would also make disagreement informative. A brewer's objection could reveal that the laboratory had measured a successful fermentation by a different standard from the one that mattered in use.

At the beginning, we seemed to be looking for a missing yeast. What we actually found was a missing distinction: between a dry starter and a fermenting drink, between recovery and absence, between a mixed preparation and an added selected culture. Once those distinctions return, the cake becomes more interesting. It is not waiting to be reduced to a strain name. It is a working arrangement whose parts become intelligible when we ask what they do together.[^c17-6]
