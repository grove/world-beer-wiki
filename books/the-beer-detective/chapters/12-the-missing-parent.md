# 12. The Missing Parent

The two soil samples were collected about seventeen meters apart. One came from beneath sycamore leaf litter; the other from ground near oak, beech and cherry laurel. The location was the Belfield campus of University College Dublin, and the collection took place in September 2021 as part of undergraduate research. Out of those samples came two strains of a yeast species that had repeatedly failed to appear in European collections of wild isolates.[^c12-1]

For a discovery connected with lager, the setting is agreeably ungrand. No famous cellar was opened. No centuries-old bottle surrendered a surviving brewer's secret. The evidence came from ordinary soil on a campus. It would help revise a story that had already crossed from European breweries to Patagonian forests and the Tibetan Plateau. The object of the search was a parent.

Lager yeast, *Saccharomyces pastorianus*, carries genetic ancestry from two species. One is *S. cerevisiae*, familiar from many alcoholic fermentations. The other is the cold-tolerant *S. eubayanus*. A hybrid brings genetic material from distinct parental lineages into the same organism. That makes its family history more complicated than a simple line of descent, particularly when chromosomes have been lost, copied or rearranged after the original encounter.

Brewers had been using lager cultures long before scientists could identify those ancestral contributions. A culture could perform usefully without providing a legible account of its ancestry. This is an important distinction in the history of fermentation: the discovery of what an organism is does not mark the beginning of people's ability to work with it. It marks a change in what investigators can explain.

The major breakthrough in identifying the missing parent was reported in 2011 by Diego Libkind and colleagues. Working with yeasts from Patagonian southern-beech forests, they described *S. eubayanus* as a species and compared its genome with lager yeast. Their draft sequence was approximately 99.5 percent identical to the non-*cerevisiae* portion of the lager yeast genome used in the comparison. The unknown ancestral component now had a close wild relative with a name.[^c12-2]

The geographical result was irresistible. A yeast associated with the history of European lager had a newly identified relative in South America. A journey seemed to demand invention: some vessel, cargo or traveler must have carried it across an ocean. Such a route might make a splendid story. The genome comparison alone did not supply the ship, the date, the cargo or even proof that the relevant historical movement began where the first modern investigators found the organism.

Discovery location and origin are different coordinates. The distinction is easy to state and remarkably easy to forget. A species found first in one region may also live elsewhere, overlooked because it was rare, because samples were sparse or because previous methods did not recover it. An organism's distribution does not wait for a journal to print its name. The initial discovery changes the research map; it does not finish drawing it.

Jian Bing and colleagues expanded that map in 2014. They reported Asian populations of *S. eubayanus*, including a Tibetan population with a closer genomic affinity to the lager-yeast component than the Patagonian population in their comparison. They argued for a Far East Asian origin hypothesis. The finding challenged an easy identification of the first discovery site with the immediate source of the lager parent.[^c12-3]

Yet replacing Patagonia with Tibet in a finished migration tale would repeat the same mistake. Genetic resemblance can tell us about relationships among the organisms sampled. A historical itinerary requires more. It also matters which part of a genome is compared. Different stretches can preserve different pieces of an ancestral population's variation, especially when mating and recombination have repeatedly reshuffled that variation before the lineage of interest was formed.

David Peris and colleagues made this point explicit in a 2016 population-genomic study. Comparing wild isolates across regions with domesticated lager hybrids, they found that no single sampled wild strain was the sole closest relative across the relevant genome. Different regions of DNA carried different relationships. They interpreted the result in terms of pre-existing variation within wild populations and a more complicated biogeographical history than a one-isolate ancestry story would allow.[^c12-4]

Think of a family collection of photographs. One cousin may resemble a grandparent around the eyes, another in the shape of the face. Naming the cousin with the strongest resemblance does not make that person the grandparent. In genetic work, the comparison is far more exact, but the warning still helps: a close living relative is not automatically the exact historical ancestor, and a relative's present address is not automatically the ancestor's address.

Even the number of hybridization events has required argument. Modern lager strains fall into major groups with differences in chromosome content. A 2015 genomic study by EmilyClare Baker and colleagues supported at least two independent origins. In 2019, Alex Salazar and colleagues used a more complete chromosome-level assembly and comparative analysis to argue for a single hybridization followed by different evolutionary trajectories. The dispute turned partly on how differences in complicated genomes should be read.[^c12-5]

This is not a reason to describe the whole ancestry as guesswork. The broad hybrid relationship is well supported. The reconstruction of particular events is a finer-grained problem. A chromosome can be present in several copies; different copies need not be identical; subsequent losses can make related descendants look surprisingly different. A complete account has to separate changes inherited from the parents from changes accumulated afterward. Better assemblies alter the evidence available for that task.

Against this background, the Irish soil samples became especially interesting. Sean Bergin and colleagues reported the two isolates, UCD646 and UCD650, in 2022. Genome analysis placed them in the broad northern lineage that includes relatives of the lager parent. Some of the lager ancestry resembled the Irish strains, while other portions were closer to Tibetan samples. Europe could no longer be described as a continent from which wild *S. eubayanus* had never been isolated.[^c12-1]

The discovery did not prove that medieval brewers obtained yeast from Ireland. Its power was more precise: an apparent absence had become an observed presence. It weakened the need to explain every European lager lineage by a recent arrival from a distant place. The historical distribution could still have been complicated. What changed was the set of explanations that the evidence required, and the urgency of looking more carefully in European habitats.

Meanwhile, historians and microbiologists were asking where the parental lineages might have met in a brewery. In 2023 Mathias Hutzler and colleagues proposed a location and interval: Munich's Hofbräuhaus between 1602 and 1615, when top-fermented wheat beer and bottom-fermented beer were being produced in the same institution. Their historical argument reversed a familiar assumption by suggesting that *S. cerevisiae* could have entered an environment where *S. eubayanus* was already present.[^c12-6]

This is an archival hypothesis, not an observed mating event. It connects documented brewing arrangements with a biologically plausible encounter. Its strength lies in the specificity of the proposed circumstances; its limit is that brewery records cannot ordinarily identify an invisible hybridization as it occurred. A shipment of yeast can be documented without its genome. A genome can preserve ancestry without preserving a shipment label.

The hypothesis also helps separate the history of cold brewing from the origin of the modern hybrid. Brewing practices can precede the particular organism now associated with them. If older brewers used mixed cultures or different cold-tolerant yeasts, a record of bottom fermentation would not automatically prove the presence of present-day *S. pastorianus*. A method's continuity and a microorganism's continuity must be investigated separately, even when both travel under the word lager.

Human activity nevertheless belongs inside the biological story. Reusing cultures gives some organisms repeated access to wort. Changing temperatures, ingredients and handling alters the conditions under which those organisms succeed. The surviving culture is therefore neither a wholly wild visitor nor a machine designed from a blank sheet. It is a living result of encounters, selection and repeated practical decisions, many made before anyone could inspect a chromosome.

The search has produced a better answer than a single dramatic arrival. It has identified a parental species, exposed geographic diversity, tested competing accounts of hybrid ancestry and connected genetic questions to particular brewing institutions. It has also shown that the next useful sample need not come from a place already famous for beer. A patch of soil can matter because the organism is there, regardless of whether anyone thought to look.

The two Irish isolates are not the original parent returned from the past. They are living relatives that make the past more intelligible. Seventeen meters of ordinary university ground widened the search. Somewhere among the samples not yet collected may be another relative, one that will force a line on the family tree to be drawn again.[^c12-7]
